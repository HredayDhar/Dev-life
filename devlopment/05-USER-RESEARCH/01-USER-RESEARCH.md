# 01 — USER RESEARCH

## 1. What Is This?

User research is the systematic investigation of target users' behaviors, needs, motivations, and obstacles through various qualitative and quantitative methods. It involves gathering data about how users interact with products, what problems they face, what goals they aim to achieve, and the context in which they operate. User research provides evidence-based insights that inform product design, development, and decision-making, ensuring solutions are grounded in real user experiences rather than assumptions.

## 2. Why Does It Matter?

User research matters because:
- It prevents building elegant solutions that nobody actually wants or needs
- It uncovers hidden pain points and opportunities that stakeholders might overlook
- It reduces the risk of product failure by validating assumptions before significant investment
- It ensures design decisions are based on real user behavior rather than stakeholder opinions
- It helps prioritize features based on actual user value and impact
- It creates empathy within the development team for the people they're building for
- It identifies usability issues early when they're cheaper and easier to fix
- It supports data-driven decision-making throughout the product lifecycle
- It helps segment users effectively to tailor solutions to different groups
- It uncovers workarounds and adaptations users have developed that reveal true needs
- It provides evidence to resolve internal debates about design direction
- It supports accessibility by identifying diverse user capabilities and contexts
- It helps measure and improve user satisfaction over time
- It reduces support costs by building more intuitive products from the start
- It creates competitive advantage through deeper user understanding
- It supports ethical product development by considering actual user impact

## 3. What Problem Does It Solve?

Without adequate user research, teams commonly experience:
- Building features based on assumptions that don't match user reality
- Missing critical workflow steps or edge cases that cause frustration
- Creating overly complex solutions for simple problems or vice versa
- Wasting development effort on low-value features users rarely use
- Launching products with poor adoption due to misaligned value propositions
- Having to perform costly redesigns after launch when fundamental flaws are discovered
- Misinterpreting user feedback due to lack of contextual understanding
- Overlooking accessibility needs that exclude significant user populations
- Failing to recognize when users have adapted problematic workarounds
- Developing solutions that create new problems while solving the original one
- Internal disagreements about user preferences that stall decision-making
- Inability to measure success because baseline user understanding is lacking
- Poor conversion rates due to unclear value propositions or confusing flows
- Increased user support burden from preventable usability issues
- Reputational damage from releasing products that frustrate or confuse users
- Missed market opportunities from not recognizing emerging user needs
- Difficulty securing stakeholder buy-in without evidence to support proposals
- Ethical concerns from deploying products that may harm or exploit users
- Technical debt from building inflexible systems that can't adapt to user feedback
- Poor retention due to solutions that don't integrate well into user lifestyles
- Ineffective marketing from messaging that doesn't resonate with target audiences

## 4. When Should We Use It?

User research should be used:
- At the beginning of any product or feature development cycle
- Before making significant design or architectural decisions
- When entering new markets or targeting new user segments
- When preparing for major redesigns or platform migrations
- When responding to declining user metrics or engagement
- When investigating specific usability problems or user complaints
- When validating assumptions about user behavior or preferences
- When exploring opportunities for innovation or differentiation
- When preparing for pricing or business model changes
- When investigating drop-off points in conversion funnels
- When preparing for regulatory or compliance changes affecting user experience
- When responding to competitive pressures or market shifts
- When planning A/B tests or experiments requiring user segmentation
- When developing training or support materials for users
- When preparing for internationalization or localization efforts
- When investigating cross-platform or multi-device usage patterns
- When preparing for accessibility improvements or audits
- When developing content strategy or information architecture
- When preparing for customer journey mapping or service design
- When investigating post-launch performance issues
- When preparing for mergers or acquisitions affecting user-facing products
- When developing metrics or analytics frameworks requiring user context

## 5. When Should We NOT Use It?

While user research is generally valuable, minimal formal research might be appropriate when:
- Addressing urgent, safety, or security critical issues requiring immediate action
- Working on purely exploratory technical spikes with no user impact
- Making minor, trivial UI tweaks with no functional change or user effect
- The user population is extremely homogeneous and well-understood from recent research
- In maintenance contexts where the goal is simply system stability with no user-facing changes
- Emergency situations where delay for research could worsen outcomes
- Well-established internal tools with fixed, known user bases and unchanged requirements
- Learning exercises or proof-of-concepts with no expectation of production use
- When working on infrastructure improvements with clear, direct technical benefits
- When the request comes from a single, clearly identified user with limited scope
- When organizational policies already define standardized approaches for certain changes
- When historical data shows consistent user behavior requiring minimal reassessment
- When researching topics with well-established scientific consensus in the domain
- When making changes based solely on technical constraints or platform requirements
- When updating legacy systems where user expectations are well documented
- When the research would duplicate recent, still-relevant studies unnecessarily
- When stakeholder alignment is the primary blocker rather than user understanding
- When regulatory requirements dictate specific implementations regardless of user preference

## 6. Core Concepts

### User Research Methods

1. **Qualitative Methods**: Exploratory research focusing on understanding meanings, experiences, and motivations
   - User interviews (structured, semi-structured, unstructured)
   - Ethnographic field studies and contextual inquiry
   - Focus groups and group discussions
   - Diary studies and experience sampling
   - Usability testing (moderated and unmoderated)
   - Card sorting and information architecture exercises
   - Journey mapping and service blueprinting
   - Participatory design and co-creation workshops
   - Open-ended surveys and questionnaires
   - Social media listening and online community analysis

2. **Quantitative Methods**: Confirmatory research focusing on measuring and statistically analyzing user behavior
   - Surveys and questionnaires with closed-ended questions
   - Analytics and behavioral data analysis (clickstreams, funnels, conversion)
   - A/B testing and multivariate testing
   - Tree testing and first-click testing
   - Click testing and eye-tracking studies
   - Desirability studies and preference testing
   - Systematic usability measurement (SUMI, SUS, NPS)
   - Longitudinal studies and cohort analysis
   - Segmentation and clustering analysis
   - Funnel analysis and conversion rate optimization
   - Heatmaps and scroll mapping
   - Form analytics and field-level interaction tracking

3. **Attitudinal vs Behavioral Research**: 
   - Attitudinal: What users say they think, feel, or would do (surveys, interviews)
   - Behavioral: What users actually do (observation, analytics, testing)

4. **Generative vs Evaluative Research**:
   - Generative (Discovery): Understanding problems and opportunities (early stage)
   - Evaluative (Validation): Testing solutions and assumptions (later stage)

5. **Remote vs In-Person Research**: 
   - Remote: Conducted via video conferencing, online tools, or unmoderated methods
   - In-Person: Conducted in shared physical space or field settings

6. **Moderated vs Unmoderated Research**:
   - Moderated: Researcher guides the session in real-time
   - Unmoderated: Participants complete tasks independently with researcher analysis later

### Research Artifacts

- **Research Plan**: Objectives, methods, participant criteria, timeline, logistics
- **Discussion Guide**: Structured questions and prompts for interviews or usability tests
- **Consent Forms**: Documentation of informed consent for participation
- **Screening Surveys**: Questionnaires to ensure participants meet criteria
- **Observation Notes**: Raw data captured during research sessions
- **Transcripts**: Verbatim records of interviews or think-aloud protocols
- **Affinity Maps**: Grouped insights from qualitative data
- **Personas**: Fictional characters representing user segments based on research
- **Journey Maps**: Visual representations of user experiences over time
- **Empathy Maps**: What users say, think, do, and feel
- **Mental Models**: How users conceptualize how something works
- **Task Analyses**: Breakdown of steps users take to accomplish goals
- **Usability Reports**: Findings from testing including severity ratings and recommendations
- **Survey Results**: Statistical analysis of questionnaire responses
- **Analytics Reports**: Interpretation of behavioral data patterns
- **Research Repository**: Organized collection of research data for future reference
- **Insights Summary**: Key findings and recommendations for stakeholders
- **Backlog Items**: Specific design or development tasks derived from research
- **Research Debrief**: Summary of sessions conducted and lessons learned

### Research Quality Considerations

- **Validity**: Does the research measure what it intends to measure?
- **Reliability**: Would the research produce similar results if repeated?
- **Generalizability**: To what extent can findings be applied to the broader user population?
- **Objectivity**: Are findings influenced by researcher bias or assumptions?
- **Ethical Soundness**: Does the research respect participant rights and well-being?
- **Contextual Accuracy**: Does the research capture the true context of use?
- **Triangulation**: Are findings supported by multiple methods or data sources?
- **Saturation**: Have we collected enough data that new insights are minimal?
- **Representativeness**: Do participants adequately reflect the target user population?
- **Timeliness**: Is the research recent enough to reflect current user behavior?
- **Actionability**: Can the findings be translated into concrete design or development decisions?

## 7. Step-by-Step Process

1. **Define Research Objectives**: Clarify what specific questions the research needs to answer
2. **Review Existing Knowledge**: Examine prior research, analytics, and stakeholder assumptions
3. **Select Appropriate Methods**: Choose qualitative, quantitative, or mixed methods based on objectives
4. **Develop Research Protocol**: Create detailed plan including recruitment, consent, and procedures
5. **Recruit Participants**: Identify and screen users matching target characteristics
6. **Conduct Research Sessions**: Execute interviews, observations, tests, or surveys according to protocol
7. **Collect and Organize Data**: Gather recordings, notes, responses, and behavioral data
8. **Analyze Qualitative Data**: Code transcripts, identify patterns, and group insights
9. **Analyze Quantitative Data**: Calculate statistics, identify trends, and test hypotheses
10. **Triangulate Findings**: Combine qualitative and quantitative insights for stronger conclusions
11. **Develop Insights and Recommendations**: Translate findings into actionable product guidance
12. **Create Research Deliverables**: Produce reports, personas, journey maps, and other artifacts
13. **Share Findings with Stakeholders**: Present research results to inform decision-making
14. **Integrate Insights into Workflow**: Feed research outcomes into design, development, and planning
15. **Archive Research Data**: Store data and methods for future reference and longitudinal studies
16. **Plan Follow-up Research**: Determine what additional questions arise from current findings
17. **Measure Research Impact**: Track how research influenced decisions and outcomes
