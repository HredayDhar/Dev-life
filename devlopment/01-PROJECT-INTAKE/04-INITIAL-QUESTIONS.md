# Initial Questions

## 1. What Is This?

Initial questions are a structured set of inquiries designed to uncover critical information about a proposed software project during the Project Intake phase. They help identify gaps in understanding, reveal assumptions that need validation, and determine what further investigation is required before proceeding to detailed problem definition and requirements gathering. These questions transform a vague idea into a well-understood concept by systematically exploring the who, what, why, when, where, and how of the project.

## 2. Why Does It Matter?

Asking the right initial questions is crucial because:
- **Assumption Identification**: Makes explicit what is being taken for granted so it can be validated or challenged
- **Gap Detection**: Reveals what important information is missing before significant resources are invested
- **Risk Early Warning**: Identifies potential showstoppers, constraints, or dependencies that could derail the project later
- **Focus Definition**: Helps narrow broad ideas into manageable, well-scoped initiatives
- **Stakeholder Alignment**: Ensures everyone involved shares a common understanding of what's being proposed
- **Efficiency Improvement**: Prevents wasted effort on investigating the wrong things or solving the wrong problems
- **Foundation for Planning**: Provides the information needed to create realistic estimates, schedules, and resource plans
- **Decision Quality**: Enables better go/no-go decisions based on comprehensive understanding rather than intuition
- **Communication Baseline**: Establishes a shared reference point for discussions among team members and stakeholders
- **Learning Acceleration**: Helps the team come up to speed quickly on the problem domain and context

## 3. What Problem Does It Solve?

Without a structured approach to initial questioning, projects often suffer from:
- **Unvalidated Assumptions**: Proceeding on false beliefs about user needs, technical feasibility, or business constraints
- **Missing Critical Information**: Overlooking key factors like regulatory requirements, integration points, or user constraints
- **Scope Misunderstanding**: Different team members having different interpretations of what the project entails
- **Late Discovery of Showstoppers**: Finding out too late that a key assumption is invalid or a dependency cannot be met
- **Inefficient Investigation**: Wasting time researching irrelevant aspects while missing critical ones
- **Poor Stakeholder Communication**: Talking past each other due to lack of shared understanding
- **Repeated Work**: Having to redo analysis because initial exploration was incomplete or misdirected
- **False Confidence**: Believing the project is well-understood when critical gaps remain
- **Solution Mismatch**: Developing something that doesn't actually address the core problem or user needs
- **Regulatory Surprises**: Discovering compliance requirements after significant work has already been done

## 4. When Should We Use It?

Initial questions should be asked:
- Immediately after a project idea is captured in the intake process
- During the earliest stakeholder conversations about the project
- When reviewing the project overview and objectives
- Before investing significant time in detailed requirements gathering or design
- When onboarding new team members to the project
- Whenever significant new information emerges that might change understanding
- As part of checkpoint reviews throughout the intake and early analysis phases
- When preparing to transition from intake to problem definition or feasibility study
- Onboarding consultants or external experts who need to quickly understand the project
- As input to risk identification and mitigation planning

## 5. When Should We NOT Use It?

You probably don't need a formal initial questioning process when:
- The work is a trivial, well-understood task with clear, unambiguous requirements
- You're fixing a specific bug with a known root cause and solution
- The effort is purely experimental with no intention of deployment or external impact
- The project duration is extremely short (hours) with immediate, localized impact
- You're performing routine maintenance on a well-understood system
- You're making a minor enhancement to an existing feature with established patterns
- You're working on a personal learning project with no external stakeholders or impact

## 6. Core Concepts

### Question Categories
Initial questions typically fall into several categories that together provide comprehensive understanding:

**Problem Context**
- What problem are we trying to solve?
- Who experiences this problem and how severely?
- What happens if we don't solve this problem?
- Is this a new problem or an existing one that's worsening?
- What evidence do we have that this problem exists and matters?

**Stakeholders and Users**
- Who are the primary users or beneficiaries of this solution?
- Who else might be affected (positively or negatively) by this project?
- Who has expertise or authority in this domain?
- Who might resist or oppose this change?
- What are the different user roles or personas involved?

**Goals and Objectives**
- What specific outcomes are we hoping to achieve?
- How will we know if the project is successful?
- What metrics or indicators will we use to measure success?
- What business goals does this project support?
- What user needs or pain points are we addressing?

**Constraints and Limitations**
- What are the hard constraints (budget, timeline, regulatory, technical)?
- What assumptions are we making that could turn out to be false?
- What dependencies do we have on other systems, teams, or external factors?
- What limitations exist on potential solutions (technology choices, platforms, etc.)
- What resources are available or unavailable for this project?

**Solution Space**
- What solutions have been considered or attempted so far?
- What are the boundaries of what we're willing to consider (build vs buy, etc.)?
- What are the non-negotiable aspects of any potential solution?
- What would constitute a "good enough" solution versus an ideal one?
- What are we explicitly NOT trying to achieve with this project?

**Implementation Context**
- Where will this solution be deployed or used?
- What existing systems or processes will it need to work with?
- What technical environments or platforms must it support?
- What are the deployment, operations, and maintenance considerations?
- What skills, knowledge, or resources does the team currently have or need?

**Risks and Unknowns**
- What are the biggest risks or uncertainties associated with this project?
- What don't we know that we need to find out?
- What could go wrong, and how likely or severe would it be?
- What information would most reduce our uncertainty about the project?
- What would make us reconsider or abandon this project?

### Characteristics of Good Initial Questions
- **Open-Ended**: Encourage elaboration rather than yes/no answers
- **Non-Leading**: Don't suggest a particular answer through phrasing
- **Specific Enough**: Focused enough to yield useful information
- **Action-Oriented**: Designed to uncover information that will inform decisions
- **Assumption-Challenging**: Help reveal what is being taken for granted
- **Context-Aware**: Appropriate to the project domain and organizational setting
- **Prioritized**: Focus on the most critical uncertainties first
- **Complementary**: Work together to build a holistic understanding
- **Traceable**: Can be linked back to specific project decisions or artifacts
- **Evolving**: Can be refined or expanded as understanding increases

### Techniques for Effective Questioning
- **5 Whys**: Iteratively asking "why" to get to root causes
- **Starbursting**: Creating questions from each point of a star (who, what, when, where, why, how)
- **Situation-Complication-Question**: Framing questions within a narrative of what's happening and what's problematic
- **Job Interviews**: Adapting behavioral interview techniques to explore experiences and motivations
- **Workshop Facilitation**: Using structured activities to generate and prioritize questions from groups
- **Question Templates**: Developing reusable question sets for common project types or domains
- **Question Storming**: Generating as many questions as possible without judgment, then refining
- **Question Classification**: Grouping questions by type to ensure comprehensive coverage
- **Answer Anticipation**: Thinking about what answers would mean for the project before asking
- **Follow-Up Planning**: Preparing probing questions based on likely responses

## 7. Step-by-Step Process

1. **Review Existing Information**: Examine the project overview, objectives, and any initial stakeholder input
2. **Brainstorm Question Areas**: Identify the key dimensions of uncertainty (problem, stakeholders, constraints, etc.)
3. **Generate Candidate Questions**: Create a broad list of potential questions without filtering
4. **Refine and Prioritize**: Edit questions for clarity, remove duplicates, and rank by importance
5. **Validate Stakeholder Relevance**: Ensure questions will yield information useful to decision-makers
- **6. Plan Questioning Approach**: Determine who to ask, when, and how (interviews, workshops, surveys, etc.)
- **7. Conduct Initial Questioning**: Gather answers through conversations, document reviews, or research
- **8. Synthesize and Analyze**: Look for patterns, contradictions, and gaps in the responses
- **9. Identify Information Gaps**: Determine what critical questions remain unanswered
- **10. Develop Investigation Plan**: Outline how to obtain missing information (research, spikes, prototypes, etc.)
- **11. Document Findings**: Record what was learned and what still needs investigation
- **12. Communicate Understanding**: Share the current state of knowledge with the team and stakeholders
- **13. Update Project Artifacts**: Revise the overview, objectives, or other documents based on new insights
- **14. Determine Next Steps**: Decide whether to proceed to problem definition, need more intake work, or reject the project
- **15. Archive Questions and Answers**: Preserve the questioning process for future reference and learning

## 8. Inputs

- Project overview (purpose, value proposition, scope)
- Project objectives (specific, measurable goals)
- Stakeholder identification and preliminary analysis
- Available domain knowledge from the project team
- Organizational context (structure, processes, culture, history)
- Market research, competitor analysis, or industry reports
- User feedback, complaints, or suggestions (if available)
- Technical assessments or feasibility studies (if any exist)
- Regulatory and compliance requirements
- Resource and budget information
- Lessons learned from similar past projects
- Available time and resources for investigation

## 9. Outputs / Deliverables

- **Initial Questions Document**: The curated list of questions to be answered
- **Question-Answer Record**: Documented responses from stakeholder interviews, research, etc.
- **Assumption Log**: Explicit list of assumptions made and their validation status
- **Information Gap Analysis': Summary of what is known, unknown, and needs investigation
- **Investigation Recommendations**: Specific proposals for obtaining missing information
- **Updated Project Understanding**: Refinements to overview, objectives, or scope based on findings
- **Risk and Dependency Identifiers**: Early identification of potential issues to monitor
- **Stakeholder Communication Summary**: What was learned from and shared with different groups
- **Go/No-Go Recommendation Basis**: Evidence to support the decision about whether to proceed
- **Input to Requirements Traceability**: Links between answered questions and emerging requirements
- **Learning Document**: Insights about the questioning process itself for future improvement

## 10. Real-World Example

**Scenario**: A retail company proposes to develop a mobile app for in-store product scanning and self-checkout.

**Initial Questions Generated**:

**Problem Context**:
- What specific shopping pain points are we trying to address? (Long checkout lines? Difficulty finding prices? Wanting product details?)
- How do we know these problems exist and are significant enough to warrant a solution?
- What evidence do we have from customer complaints, store observations, or market research?
- Are we trying to solve a problem for customers, store employees, or both?
- What happens if we don't implement this solution? Will customers go to competitors? Will store efficiency suffer?

**Stakeholders and Users**:
- Who are the primary users? (Customers? Store associates? Both?)
- What different customer segments might use this? (Tech-savvy millennials? All age groups? Only frequent shoppers?)
- Who in the store organization would be affected? (Cashiers? Floor staff? Managers? Loss prevention?)
- What external parties might be involved? (Payment processors? Bag suppliers? Loyalty program partners?)
- Who might resist this change? (Traditionalist customers? Employees worried about job security?)
- Who has expertise we need to consult? (Checkout system vendors? Security experts? Accessibility specialists?)

**Goals and Objectives**:
- What specific outcomes would make this project successful? (Reduce average checkout time by X%? Increase customer satisfaction scores?)
- How will we measure success? (Transaction timing? Survey results? Store employee feedback?)
- What business goals does this support? (Increase store throughput? Reduce labor costs? Improve customer loyalty?)
- What would be a "nice to have" versus a "must have" outcome?
- Are we trying to replace traditional checkout completely or supplement it?

**Constraints and Limitations**:
- What is the budget range for this project?
- What is the desired timeline for launch?
- What technical constraints exist? (Must work with existing POS system? Support specific mobile platforms?)
- What regulatory constraints exist? (Age-restricted items? Payment processing regulations? Data privacy laws?)
- What assumptions are we making? (That customers have smartphones? That stores have adequate Wi-Fi?)
- What dependencies do we have? (On POS vendor APIs? On payment gateway compliance? On store IT resources?)
- What would make this project infeasible or not worth pursuing?

**Solution Space**:
- Have we considered alternatives to a custom mobile app? (Using existing retailer apps? Implementing scan-and-go with store-provided devices?)
- What features are absolutely essential? (Scanning? Payment? Receipt generation? Loyalty integration?)
- What would we explicitly NOT include in this first version? (Social sharing? Complex product recommendations? Augmented reality features?)
- What would constitute a minimum viable product versus a fully featured solution?
- Are we open to purchasing and customizing an existing solution rather than building from scratch?

**Implementation Context**:
- Where will customers use this? (Throughout the store? Only in specific departments? At designated checkout areas?)
- What existing systems will it need to integrate with? (POS? Inventory? Payment? Loyalty? Security systems?)
- What mobile platforms must we support? (iOS? Android? Specific versions?)
- What network conditions should we assume? (Store Wi-Fi quality? Fallback to cellular?)
- What are the deployment considerations? (App store distribution? Enterprise distribution? Updates?)
- What operations and maintenance will be required? (Who will support it? How will updates be handled?)
- What accessibility requirements must we meet? (Screen reader support? Alternative input methods? Color contrast?)
- What security and fraud prevention considerations exist? (How do we prevent unauthorized item removal? How do we verify age-restricted purchases?)

**Risks and Unknowns**:
- What are the biggest risks? (Low customer adoption? Technical integration failures? Increased shoplifting?)
- What don't we know that we critically need to find out? (Actual customer willingness to use? Accurate integration effort estimates?)
- What information would most reduce our uncertainty? (A pilot test with real customers? A technical spike on POS integration?)
- What would make us reconsider this project? (Prohibitive integration costs? Legal barriers? Evidence that customers prefer current checkout?)
- What assumptions are we making that, if wrong, would sink the project? (That stores have reliable Wi-Fi throughout? That payment processing will work seamlessly?)

**How the Questions Were Used**:
- The project team conducted interviews with store managers, customers, and IT staff
- They observed checkout processes in several stores to understand pain points
- They researched existing self-checkout solutions and mobile payment technologies
- They created assumption logs tracking things like "customers have smartphones" and "store Wi-Fi is adequate"
- Answers revealed that: 
  - Primary pain point was indeed long lines, especially during peak hours
  - Customers wanted both speed and the ability to see running totals
  - Store associates were concerned about theft prevention but saw potential for reducing mundane tasks
  - Integration with the legacy POS system would be challenging but possible through middleware
  - The biggest unknown was actual customer adoption rate, which they addressed through a small pilot
  - Assumptions about smartphone ownership were validated (95% of target demographic had smartphones)
  - Assumptions about store Wi-Fi proved incorrect in older locations, leading to hybrid Wi-Fi/cellular approach
- Based on findings, the project overview was refined to focus on "scan and pay" functionality with integrated theft prevention
- Objectives were set around reducing average checkout time, increasing lanes throughput, and maintaining shrinkage rates
- The team proceeded to problem definition with confidence in their understanding of the core problem and constraints

## 11. Technical Example

**Scenario**: A financial institution wants to improve its fraud detection system for online banking transactions.

**Initial Questions Generated**:

**Problem Context**:
- What specific types of fraud are we trying to prevent? (Account takeover? Fraudulent transactions? Identity theft?)
- How do we currently detect and respond to fraud, and what are the limitations of those methods?
- What evidence do we have that fraud is a significant and growing problem?
- What fraud attempts are we missing entirely with current systems?
- What false positives are we generating that negatively impact legitimate customers?
- What is the financial impact of fraud (direct losses plus operational costs)?
- Are we trying to solve a known problem or get ahead of emerging threats?

**Stakeholders and Users**:
- Who are the primary users of the current system? (Fraud analysts? Investigators? Automated systems?)
- Who experiences the negative impacts of fraud? (Customers? The bank? Merchants?)
- Who would be involved in or affected by changes to the fraud detection system?
- Who has expertise in fraud patterns, detection techniques, or relevant regulations?
- Who might resist changes to the system? (Analysts comfortable with current methods? Teams worried about false positives?)
- Who are the external parties we might need to coordinate with? (Payment networks? Law enforcement? Other banks?)
- Who are the regulatory bodies that oversee fraud prevention practices?

**Goals and Objectives**:
- What specific fraud reduction targets are we aiming for? (Reduce fraud losses by X%? Detect Y% more fraudulent transactions?)
- How will we measure success? (Fraud loss amounts? Detection rates? False positive rates? Investigator efficiency?)
- What balance are we seeking between fraud detection and legitimate transaction approval?
- What are the acceptable trade-offs between catching more fraud and inconveniencing good customers?
- How do we define success in terms of both stopping fraud and maintaining customer experience?
- What timeframe are we considering for achieving these objectives?
- Are we trying to meet specific regulatory requirements or industry standards?

**Constraints and Limitations**:
- What is the budget allocated for this initiative?
- What is the desired timeline for implementation and results?
- What technical constraints exist? (Must work with existing transaction processing systems? Support specific data formats?)
- What regulatory constraints apply? (Data privacy laws? Financial regulations? Reporting requirements?)
- What assumptions are we making? (About fraud patterns? About data quality? About analyst availability?)
- What dependencies do we have? (On data quality? On specific third-party services? On analyst expertise?)
- What limitations exist on potential solutions? (Real-time vs batch processing? Explainability requirements?)
- What would make this project infeasible or not worth pursuing from a business perspective?

**Solution Space**:
- Have we considered alternative approaches to improving fraud detection? (Rule tweaks? Model retraining? Third-party services?)
- What aspects of the current system are we willing to change? (Data inputs? Scoring models? Alert thresholds?)
- What would we explicitly NOT change in this initiative? (Core transaction flow? Legal compliance procedures?)
- What would constitute an incremental improvement versus a transformative solution?
- Are we open to outsourcing certain aspects rather than building everything in-house?
- What are we willing to invest in terms of false positives to catch more fraud (or vice versa)?

**Implementation Context**:
- Where in the transaction flow would fraud detection occur? (Pre-authorization? Post-authorization? Both?)
- What data sources would we need to access or integrate? (Transaction data? User behavior? Device fingerprinting? External threat intelligence?)
- What technical environments must we support? (Mainframe? Distributed systems? Cloud platforms?)
- What performance requirements exist? (Real-time decision making? Latency limits? Throughput requirements?)
- What are the deployment considerations? (How do we roll out changes without disrupting service?)
- What operations and maintenance will be required? (Who will monitor the system? How will models be updated?)
- What skills, knowledge, or resources does the fraud team currently have or need to develop?
- What audit trail and reporting requirements must we meet for regulators and internal stakeholders?
- What are the considerations for handling different transaction types (credit vs debit, domestic vs international)?
- What are the requirements for dealing with deferred or batch-processed transactions?

**Risks and Unknowns**:
- What are the biggest risks? (Increased false positives leading to customer dissatisfaction? Missing new fraud vectors?)
- What don't we know that we critically need to find out? (Actual effectiveness of different approaches? Integration effort with legacy systems?)
- What information would most reduce our uncertainty? (A controlled test of candidate approaches on historical data?
- What would make us reconsider this project? (Prohibitive costs? Technical infeasibility? Evidence that current approaches are near optimal?)
- What assumptions are we making that, if wrong, would undermine the project? (That fraud patterns are stable enough to model? That we can get the needed data quality?)
- What early warning indicators would tell us we're on the wrong track.

**How the Questions Were Used**:
- The analytics team conducted interviews with fraud investigators, transaction processing engineers, and compliance officers
- They analyzed historical fraud data to understand patterns, losses, and current detection rates
- They researched industry benchmarks and emerging fraud detection technologies
- They created assumption logs tracking things like "we have sufficient historical data" and "transaction features are predictive"
- Answers revealed that:
  - The primary fraud types were account takeover (via credential stuffing) and new account fraud
  - Current rule-based system was missing sophisticated, low-velocity fraud patterns
  - Investigators were spending excessive time on false positives from overly broad rules
  - Integration with the transaction processing system would require significant middleware development
  - The biggest unknown was the actual uplift in detection rate achievable with machine learning approaches
  - Assumptions about data quality were largely validated, but some key behavioral features were noisy or missing
  - Assumptions about real-time processing feasibility were challenged by legacy system latency
- Based on findings, the project was refined to focus on a hybrid approach: enhance existing rules for high-volume fraud while adding ML models for sophisticated patterns
- Objectives were set around reducing fraud losses, maintaining false positive rates below a threshold, and reducing investigator workload per case
- The team proceeded to problem definition with a clear understanding of the fraud landscape, technical constraints, and opportunity space

## 12. Good Approach

- **Start Broad, Then Narrow**: Begin with wide-ranging questions to understand the landscape, then focus on critical uncertainties
- **Question the Questions**: Regularly review whether your questions are actually yielding the information you need
- **Trace Questions to Decisions**: Link each question to a specific project decision or artifact it will inform
- **Balance Breadth and Depth**: Have enough questions to cover important areas but not so many that the process becomes unwieldy
- **Prioritize Ruthlessly**: Focus on questions whose answers would most significantly affect project decisions
- **Use Different Question Types**: Mix open-ended, scenario-based, and assumption-challenging questions for comprehensive insight
- **Validate Assumptions Explicitly**: Don't just ask questions—create a separate assumption log to track what you're taking for granted
- **Consider the Answer Before Asking**: Think about what different possible answers would mean for the project
- **Tailor to Audience**: Phrase questions appropriately for different stakeholder groups (executives vs technicians vs users)
- **Leverage Existing Knowledge**: Don't ask questions whose answers you already know or can easily look up
- **Plan for Follow-Up**: Prepare probing questions based on likely or desirable answers
- **Document the Process**: Record not just the answers but how you arrived at the questions and who you asked
- **Watch for Patterns**: Look for contradictory answers that reveal deeper issues or uncertainties
- **Think About Scale**: Consider how answers might change at different levels of implementation or adoption
- **Mind the Timing**: Some questions are only relevant at certain project stages—don't ask about UI colors during feasibility analysis
- **Embrace Constructive Tension**: Welcome questions that reveal disagreements or different perspectives
- **Close the Loop**: Let question-askers know how their input was used or why certain questions weren't pursued
- **Learn from the Process**: Refine your questioning technique based on what worked and didn't in this project

## 13. Bad Approach

- **Asking Leading Questions**: Phrasing questions in a way that suggests a particular answer ("Don't you think we should...?")
- **Focusing Only on the Positive**: Ignoring questions about risks, problems, or potential failures
- **Asking Irrelevant Questions**: Wasting time on details that won't affect project decisions or outcomes
- **Being Too Vague**: Questions so broad they elicit useless or unfocused responses ("Tell me about the project...")
- **Being Too Narrow**: Questions so specific they miss the bigger picture or important context
- **Assuming You Know the Answers**: Not listening carefully because you think you already know what they'll say
- **Asking Only Closed Questions**: Relying solely on yes/no questions that don't elicit explanatory information
- **Ignoring Context**: Forgetting that the meaning of answers depends on the situation, history, and relationships
- **One-and-Done**: Asking questions once and never revisiting them as understanding evolves
- **Failing to Follow Up**: Not probing deeper when interesting or potentially important answers emerge
- **Lacking a Question Hierarchy**: Treating all questions as equally important when some are clearly more critical
- **Not Linking to Decisions**: Failing to ask how the answers will actually be used to shape the project
- **Overlooking Silent Agreement**: Mistaking lack of objection for agreement or enthusiasm
- **Failing to Challenge Assumptions**: Not asking questions that would reveal what is being taken for granted
- **Asking Only What You Want to Hear**: Avoiding questions that might yield uncomfortable or inconvenient truths
- **Using Jargon or Acronyms**: Assuming everyone understands technical terms or internal abbreviations
- **Not Adapting to Responses**: Sticking rigidly to a script instead of following interesting conversational threads
- **Failing to Record Context**: Not noting who answered, when, or under what circumstances
- **Treating Answers as Truth**: Accepting responses at face value without considering bias, incomplete knowledge, or changing circumstances
- **Neglecting Non-Verbal Cues**: Missing important information conveyed through tone, hesitation, or body language
- **Overcomplicating the Process**: Making question-asking so bureaucratic that stakeholders avoid participating
- **Underestimating Effort**: Not allocating sufficient time for thoughtful questioning and active listening
- **Failing to Synthesize**: Not looking for patterns, contradictions, or insights across different question-answer pairs
- **Ignoring the Question-Asker's Bias**: Not recognizing how your own perspectives shape which questions you ask and how you interpret answers

## 14. Common Mistakes

- **Starting Too Late**: Begging questions after significant work has already begun based on assumptions
- **Relying on Secondhand Information**: Getting answers from intermediaries instead of primary sources when possible
- **Using Outdated Information**: Basing decisions on old data without verifying current conditions
- **Confusing Symptoms with Root Causes**: Addressing surface issues without understanding underlying problems
- **Neglecting Power Dynamics**: Not considering how organizational politics affect who speaks and what they say
- **Overlooking Changing Conditions**: Assuming answers will remain valid when the business, technical, or regulatory environment shifts
- **Ignoring the Question-Asker's Influence**: Not recognizing how the person asking questions can affect the answers received
- **Assuming Homogeneity**: Treating all members of a group as having identical perspectives when they may vary widely
- **Failing to Follow Conventional Wisdom**: Ignoring established practices for effective interviewing and questioning
- **Making Questions Too Formal**: Creating such a rigid process that stakeholders feel uncomfortable or guarded
- **Being Too Informal**: Lacking sufficient structure to ensure comprehensive coverage and comparability
- **Neglecting Cultural Differences**: Not adapting questioning style for different communication norms or business cultures
- **Failing to Validate Understanding**: Not checking back with sources to ensure you interpreted their answers correctly
- **Over-Reliance on Templates**: Using generic question sets without adapting them to the specific project context
- **Ignoring the Emotional Dimension**: Not recognizing that stakeholder responses may be driven by fear, excitement, or past experiences
- **Not Considering Timing**: Some questions are only relevant at certain times (e.g., pre-holiday vs post-holiday retail)
- **Failing to Close the Loop**: Not informing stakeholders how their input was used or why certain suggestions weren't adopted
- **Treating Questions as One-Way**: Not seeing questioning as an opportunity to build relationships and shared understanding
- **Neglecting Question Evolution**: Not updating questions as the project progresses and initial uncertainties are resolved
- **Using the Wrong Medium**: Trying to have complex discussions via email when face-to-face would be more effective
- **Overloading Individuals**: Asking too many questions of one person instead of distributing the inquiry across sources
- **Failing to Distinguish Fact from Opinion**: Not clarifying whether answers are based on data, experience, or personal belief
- **Missing the Interplay**: Not considering how answers to different questions relate to or affect each other
- **Neglecting to Celebrate Learning**: Not recognizing the value of eliminating possibilities or confirming hypotheses
- **Failing to Question the Questions Themselves**: Not meta-cognitively examining whether your questioning approach is effective

## 15. Security Considerations

- **Security-Focused Questions**: Include specific questions about data sensitivity, threat models, regulatory requirements, and security controls
- **Assumption Validation**: Explicitly question assumptions about security (e.g., "Is this data really public?" or "Do we actually need to encrypt this?")
- **Threat Identification**: Ask questions that help uncover potential attack vectors, abuse cases, or adversarial motivations
- **Compliance Clarification**: Determine exactly which standards, regulations, or certifications apply and what they require
- **Data Flow Understanding**: Ask about how data moves through the system, where it's stored, and who has access to it
- **Third-Party and Dependency Scrutiny**: Question the security posture of libraries, services, or platforms the project depends on
- **Incident Response Preparedness**: Ask about plans for detecting, responding to, and learning from security incidents
- **Security Awareness and Training**: Determine what security knowledge stakeholders possess or need
- **Audit and Assessment Requirements**: Understand what evidence will be needed for security reviews or penetration tests
- **Security Trade-offs**: Explicitly ask about balances between security and other concerns-house (usability, performance, development speed)
- **Vulnerability Disclosure Processes**: Understand how security issues will be reported, tracked, and resolved
- **Security Monitoring and Logging**: Ask about what security-relevant events will be captured and how they'll be used
- **Security Question Prioritization**: Focus on questions whose answers would most significantly affect security outcomes
- **Anonymous Reporting Channels**: Consider whether stakeholders might need anonymous ways to report security concerns
- **Security Question Validation**: Validate your understanding of security issues with experts or through practical testing
- **Evolving Threat Landscape**: Recognize that security questions and answers may change as new threats emerge
- **Security Question Documentation**: Keep detailed records of security-related questions and answers for future reference
- **Cross-Domain Security Thinking**: Apply security thinking to questions that might not initially seem security-related

## 16. Performance Considerations

- **Performance-Focused Questions**: Include specific questions about speed, throughput, resource utilization, and scalability expectations
- **Baseline Understanding**: Ask about current performance levels to set realistic improvement goals
- **User-Perceived vs Technical Performance**: Distinguish between what the system measures and what users actually experience
- **Load Conditions and Patterns**: Question about expected usage patterns (normal load, peak load, seasonal variations, etc.)
- **Performance Assumption Validation**: Explicitly question assumptions about current performance, bottlenecks, or improvement potential
- **Performance Trade-offs**: Ask about balances between performance and other concerns (features, correctness, development speed)
- **Measurement and Instrumentation**: Determine how performance will be measured, what tools will be used, and what data will be collected
- **Baseline Establishment**: Ensure agreement on what constitutes current performance before setting improvement targets
- **Scalability Questions**: Include specific questions about expected growth in users, data volume, or transaction rates
- **Performance Bottleneck Identification**: Ask about known or suspected performance limitations in existing systems
- **Performance Dependency Scrutiny**: Question the performance characteristics of libraries, services, or platforms the project depends on
- **Real-Time vs Batch Processing**: Clarify whether performance requirements apply to individual transactions or batch processes
- **User Experience Metrics**: Ask about how performance relates to user satisfaction, task completion rates, or abandonment rates
- **Infrastructure and Hosting Considerations**: Question about the performance capabilities of deployment environments
- **Monitoring and Alerting Thresholds**: Determine what performance metrics will trigger alerts or require investigation
- **Performance Question Prioritization**: Focus on questions whose answers would most significantly affect performance outcomes
- **Performance Question Validation**: Validate your understanding of performance issues through testing, benchmarking, or prototyping
- **Evolving Performance Context**: Recognize that performance questions and answers may change as usage patterns or technologies shift
- **Performance Question Documentation**: Keep detailed records of performance-related questions and answers for future reference
- **Cross-Domain Performance Thinking**: Apply performance thinking to questions that might not initially seem performance-related

## 17. Scalability Considerations

- **Scalability-Focused Questions**: Include specific questions about expected growth in users, data, transactions, or geographic reach
- **Growth Projection Sources**: Question where estimates of future scale come from and how reliable they are
- **Scalability Assumption Validation**: Explicitly question assumptions about current usage, growth rates, or system limitations
- **Different Scalability Dimensions**: Ask about scaling in terms of user count, data size, request rate, geographic distribution, etc.
- **Scalability Trade-offs**: Ask about balances between scalability and other concerns (consistency, performance, cost, development speed)
- **Scalability Measurement and Validation**: Determine how scalability will be tested, measured, and confirmed
- **Scalability Dependency Scrutiny**: Question the scalability characteristics of libraries, services, or platforms the project depends on
- **Architectural Implications**: Ask about how scalability requirements might influence technical architecture decisions
- **Scalability Timing Questions**: Clarify when scalability becomes critical (immediate need vs future planning)
- **Scalability Resource Implications**: Question about the financial, human, or infrastructural resources needed to achieve scalability
- **Scalability Failure Modes**: Ask about what could prevent the system from scaling as expected and how likely those are
- **Scalability Question Prioritization**: Focus on questions whose answers would most significantly affect scalability outcomes
- **Scalability Question Validation**: Validate your understanding of scalability issues through load testing, modeling, or prototyping
- **Evolving Scalability Context**: Recognize that scalability questions and answers may change as business goals or technologies shift
- **Scalability Question Documentation**: Keep detailed records of scalability-related questions and answers for future reference
- **Cross-Domain Scalability Thinking**: Apply scalability thinking to questions that might not initially seem scalability-related
- **Scalability and Elasticity Distinction**: Understand the difference between handling predicted growth (scalability) and handling variable load (elasticity)
- **Geographic Scalability Questions**: Include specific questions about latency, data sovereignty, and regional compliance considerations
- **Data Growth Scalability Questions**: Ask about expectations for data volume growth and implications for storage and querying
- **User Growth Scalability Questions**: Question about expectations for user base growth and implications for support and licensing
- **Transaction Rate Scalability Questions**: Ask about expectations for increases in request or transaction volume
- **Platform and Technology Scalability Questions**: Question about the scalability of specific technologies, frameworks, or infrastructure choices

## 18. Maintainability Considerations

- **Maintainability-Focused Questions**: Include specific questions about understandability, modifiability, testability, and operational simplicity
- **Maintainability Assumption Validation**: Explicitly question assumptions about current code quality, documentation, or ease of change
- **Different Maintainability Dimensions**: Ask about aspects like code clarity, dependency management, test coverage, and deployment simplicity
- **Maintainability Trade-offs**: Ask about balances between maintainability and other concerns (features, performance, development speed)

- **Technical Debt Identification**: Ask about known or suspected quality issues, shortcuts, or workarounds in existing systems
- **Knowledge Transfer and Documentation**: Question about what documentation exists and what will be needed for future maintainers
- **Change and Release Processes**: Ask about how changes will be made, tested, and deployed to production
- **Operational Simplicity**: Question about the ease of monitoring, troubleshooting, and maintaining the system in production
- **Debugging and Diagnostics Capability**: Ask about what tools and information will be available for troubleshooting issues
- **Testing and Quality Assurance Processes**: Question about how tests will be written, maintained, and executed to ensure quality
- **Dependency Management Practices**: Ask about how external libraries, frameworks, and services will be tracked and updated
- **Documentation Currency and Availability**: Question about how documentation will be kept up-to-date with system changes
- **Review and Inspection Processes**: Question about how code will be reviewed to ensure quality before integration
- **Reflection and Learning Processes**: Question about how the team will learn from mistakes and improve over time
- **Maintainability Question Prioritization**: Focus on questions whose answers would most significantly affect maintainability outcomes
- **Maintainability Question Validation**: Validate your understanding of maintainability issues through code reviews, refactoring exercises, or maintenance simulations
- **Evolving Maintainability Context**: Recognize that maintainability questions and answers may change as technologies or practices shift
- **Maintainability Question Documentation**: Keep detailed records of maintainability-related questions and answers for future reference
- **Cross-Domain Maintainability Thinking**: Apply maintainability thinking to questions that might not initially seem maintainability-related
- **Legacy System Considerations**: Include specific questions about how the project will interact with or replace existing systems
- **Maintainability vs. Maintenance Distinction**: Understand that maintainability is about ease of change, while maintenance is about the actual changes made

## 19. Junior Developer Approach

When working with initial questions as a junior developer:
- Focus on understanding why certain questions are being asked and what information they're trying to uncover
- Learn to distinguish between different types of questions (problem-focused, stakeholder-focused, constraint-focused, etc.)
- Ask questions to clarify any unclear or ambiguous questions themselves
- Use the questioning process to guide your learning—understand what you need to know to contribute effectively
- Respect the time and expertise of those you're questioning—be prepared and listen actively
- Recognize that good questioning prevents wasted effort and leads to better solutions
- See how the answers to initial questions influence requirements, design, and technical decisions
- Begin to think about how you would formulate good initial questions if you were leading a project
- Understand that questioning is a skill that improves with practice, feedback, and reflection
- Value the insights that come from realizing what you don't know or had assumed incorrectly
- Appreciate that the questioning process is as important as the answers themselves for building shared understanding

## 20. Senior Developer Approach

When working with or formulating initial questions as a senior developer:
- Look beyond surface questions to understand the deeper uncertainties and risks they're trying to uncover
- Evaluate whether the question set is comprehensive enough to cover critical project dimensions
- Consider how the questions should evolve as understanding increases and new uncertainties emerge
- Think about how initial questioning should influence the overall project approach and methodology
- Use questioning as a tool to surface and resolve disagreements or different perspectives among stakeholders
- Help translate the insights from initial questions into concrete project artifacts (overview, objectives, etc.)
- Recognize that formulating good questions is a skill that requires practice, empathy, and neural plasticity
- Ensure questions are balanced across different dimensions and don't overly focus on one area to the neglect of others
- Consider the cognitive load—overly long or complex question sets may discourage thorough engagement
- Think about how the questioning process itself affects team dynamics and stakeholder relationships
- Use questioning to mentor junior developers on how to think critically and identify assumptions
- Consider the long-term value—how will the questioning process and its documentation help future projects?

## 22. Practical Exercise

**Initial Questions Workshop**:

1. **Choose a Project**: Select a real or hypothetical project (e.g., "a city implementing a smart parking system with real-time availability detection and mobile payment").

2. **Question Brainstorming**:
   - List as many questions as you can think of that would help you understand this project better
   - Don't worry about filtering, organizing, or judging the questions yet—just get them down
   - Think about the problem context, stakeholders, goals, constraints, solution space, implementation context, and risks
   - Use question starters like "What is...", "Who...", "How...", "Why...", "When...", "Where..." to prompt different types of questions
   - Consider different question types: assumption-challenging, scenario-based, open-ended, exploratory, etc.

3. **Question Categorization**:
   - Group your questions by category (problem context, stakeholders/users, goals/objectives, constraints/limitations, etc.)
   - Consider creating subcategories for particularly large or complex categories
   - Note any questions that don't fit neatly into your categories or that span multiple categories
   - Think about the purpose of each question: what decision or understanding will it inform?

4. **Question Refinement**:
   - Review each question for clarity, specificity, and usefulness
   - Remove questions that are leading, vague, duplicative, or unlikely to yield useful information
   - Refine questions to be more open-ended, specific, or action-oriented as needed
   - Ensure questions are appropriately tailored to who will be answering them (executives vs technicians vs users)
   - Think about what different possible answers would mean for the project and whether the question captures that
   - Consider the effort required to answer each question versus the value of the information gained

5. **Prioritization and Selection**:
   - Rank questions based on their potential impact on project decisions and understanding
   - Identify the most critical questions that must be answered to make an informed go/no-go decision
   - Group questions into tiers: essential for initial understanding, important for planning, nice to have for completeness
   - Aim for a manageable number of questions (typically 15-25) that cover the most important uncertainties
   - Think about the logical order in which questions should be asked—some may depend on answers to others

6. **Investigation Planning**:
   - For each selected question, determine how you would go about answering it
   - Identify potential sources of information (stakeholder interviews, document reviews, research, prototypes, etc.)
   - Estimate the effort required to answer each question and the likely sources of that information
   - Consider whether some questions might be answered in parallel while others need to be sequential
   - Think about what you would do if you couldn't get a definitive answer to a particular question
   - Plan for how you would handle contradictory or unclear answers from different sources

7. **Documentation and Sharing**:
   - Decide how you will record the questions, answers, and any assumptions made or validated
   - Consider formats that will be useful for the project team (shared document, wiki entries, cards, etc.)
   - Plan for how you will share the current state of understanding with the team and stakeholders
   - Think about how you will update the documentation as new information emerges or questions are answered
   - Consider how you will use the questioning process to inform other project artifacts like the overview or objectives
   - Think about how you will preserve the questioning process itself for future reference and learning

8. **Reflection and Learning**:
   - How did this questioning process change your understanding of what the project should achieve?
   - What was most challenging about formulating good initial questions?
   - How would these questions influence your approach to requirements gathering, design, and implementation?
   - What strategies would you use to ensure effective questioning throughout the project lifecycle?
   - How would you adapt this approach for different types of projects or organizational contexts?

## 23. Definition of Done

The initial questioning process is considered complete when:
- [ ] A comprehensive set of initial questions has been generated through systematic brainstorming and refinement
- [ ] Questions cover all critical dimensions of the project (problem, stakeholders, goals, constraints, solution space, implementation, risks)
- [ ] Questions are clear, specific, open-ended, and designed to elicit useful information for project decisions
- [ ] Duplicative, leading, or irrelevant questions have been removed or refined
- [ ] Questions have been prioritized based on their potential impact on understanding and decision-making
- [ ] Answers have been gathered for the selected critical questions through appropriate methods (interviews, research, etc.)
- [ ] Assumptions have been identified, questioned, and validated or documented as unvalidated
- [ ] Information gaps have been identified and plans developed to obtain missing critical information
- [ ] The project overview, objectives, or other artifacts have been updated based on new understanding
- [ ] The questioning process has been documented in a way that is accessible and useful to the project team
- [ ] Lessons from the questioning process have been captured for future improvement
- [ ] A determination has been made about whether to proceed to problem definition, gather more intake information, or reject the project
- [ ] The initial questions and answers have been archived for future reference and learning
