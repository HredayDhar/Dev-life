# 02 — STAKEHOLDER ANALYSIS

## 1. What Is This?

Stakeholder analysis is the systematic process of examining and evaluating the interests, influence, attitudes, expectations, and potential impact of identified stakeholders on a project, product, or initiative. Building upon stakeholder identification, this step involves assessing each stakeholder's characteristics to understand their significance, predict their behavior, and determine appropriate engagement strategies. Stakeholder analysis transforms a simple list of names into actionable insights that inform decision-making, risk management, and communication planning throughout the project lifecycle.

## 2. Why Does It Matter?

Stakeholder analysis matters because:
- It moves beyond mere identification to understanding what stakeholders actually care about and how they might affect the project
- It enables prediction of stakeholder behavior and potential reactions to project decisions or changes
- It helps prioritize engagement efforts by identifying which stakeholders require the most attention
- It reveals potential conflicts and alliances between different stakeholder groups
- It informs development of targeted communication and engagement strategies
- It supports risk identification by highlighting stakeholders who could impede project success
- It uncovers opportunities for leverage through supportive stakeholders who can champion the initiative
- It ensures resource allocation aligns with stakeholder importance and influence levels
- It enables proactive management of expectations rather than reactive problem-solving
- It supports ethical practice by ensuring engagement approaches respect stakeholder characteristics
- It provides baseline metrics for measuring changes in stakeholder attitudes over time
- It supports decision-making by incorporating stakeholder perspectives into trade-off analyses

## 3. What Problem Does It Solve?

Without thorough stakeholder analysis, teams commonly experience:
- Misallocation of engagement resources on low-impact stakeholders while neglecting critical ones
- Unexpected opposition from stakeholders whose concerns were not anticipated or understood
- Communication failures due to mismatched messaging or inappropriate channels
- Missed opportunities to leverage supportive stakeholders as advocates or champions
- Inability to predict stakeholder reactions to project milestones or decisions
- Poor risk management from unidentified sources of resistance or obstruction
- Ineffective conflict resolution due to misunderstanding underlying interests or motivations
- Wasted effort on stakeholder engagement activities that don't address core concerns
- Ethical missteps from applying one-size-fits-all approaches to diverse stakeholder groups
- Strategic blind spots from failing to consider how stakeholder dynamics affect project viability
- Reputation damage from tone-deaf engagements that fail to respect stakeholder perspectives
- Decision regrets from choosing approaches that later prove unacceptable to key constituencies
- Project delays from belated discovery of stakeholder requirements or objections
- Budget overruns from emergency stakeholder management activities
- Legal challenges from stakeholders claiming inadequate consultation or consideration

## 4. When Should We Use It?

Stakeholder analysis should be used:
- Immediately after stakeholder identification is complete, before detailed planning begins
- When preparing engagement or communication plans for the project
- When assessing project risks and developing mitigation strategies
- When determining resource allocation for stakeholder management activities
- When preparing for presentations or workshops involving multiple stakeholder groups
- When evaluating potential project alternatives or design options
- When anticipating significant changes that might affect stakeholder interests
- When preparing regulatory submissions requiring evidence of stakeholder consideration
- When developing change management plans for organizational transitions
- When preparing investment proposals requiring demonstration of stakeholder support
- When conducting competitive analysis that affects multiple stakeholder groups
- When planning organizational restructuring that impacts employees or customers
- When preparing for crisis management scenarios requiring broad stakeholder coordination
- When developing corporate social responsibility initiatives requiring community input
- When preparing public consultations or hearings requiring structured stakeholder input

## 5. When Should We NOT Use It?

While stakeholder analysis is generally valuable, minimal formal analysis might be appropriate when:
- Addressing urgent, safety, or security critical issues requiring immediate action (though even then, rapid analysis should occur)
- Working on purely exploratory research with no external impact or data collection
- Making minor, trivial changes with no user effect or external visibility
- The stakeholder landscape is extremely stable, well-documented, and unchanged from recent similar projects with known dynamics
- In maintenance contexts where the goal is simply system stability with no functional changes
- Emergency situations where delay for comprehensive analysis could worsen outcomes
- Well-established internal tools with fixed, known user bases and predictable stakeholder responses
- Learning exercises or proof-of-concepts with no expectation of production use or external impact
- When working on infrastructure improvements with clear, direct, and limited stakeholders with well-understood positions
- When the request comes from a single, clearly identified stakeholder with limited scope and predictable response
- When organizational policies explicitly define standardized engagement approaches for certain types of initiatives
- When historical data shows consistent stakeholder behavior for similar initiatives requiring minimal reassessment

## 6. Core Concepts

### Stakeholder Analysis Models

1. **Power/Interest Grid**: Plots stakeholders on a 2x2 matrix based on their power to influence and interest in the project outcomes
2. **Influence/Impact Grid**: Assesses stakeholders by their ability to influence project outcomes and degree to which they're affected by the project
3. **Salience Model**: Evaluates stakeholders based on three attributes: power (ability to influence), legitimacy (appropriateness of involvement), and urgency (need for immediate attention)
4. **Stakeholder Cube**: Three-dimensional model using power, interest, and attitude (supportive/neutral/opposed)
5. **Force Field Analysis**: Identifies driving forces (supporting change) and restraining forces (resisting change) for stakeholder positions
6. **Stakeholder Mapping**: Visual techniques showing relationships, alliances, and conflicts between stakeholders
7. **Interest vs Influence Matrix**: Similar to power/interest but focuses specifically on stakeholder interest versus actual influence capacity
8. **Support/Opposition Matrix**: Evaluates stakeholders based on their level of support for and opposition to the initiative
9. **Knowledge/Need Matrix**: Assesses stakeholders by their expertise in relevant domains and their need for project outcomes
10. **Urgency/Capacity Matrix**: Evaluates stakeholders by the urgency of their claims and their capacity to engage effectively

### Analytical Dimensions

- **Power/Authority**: Ability to sanction resources, make decisions, or enforce compliance
- **Influence**: Capacity to affect opinions, decisions, or actions of other stakeholders
- **Interest/Concern**: Degree to which stakeholder cares about project outcomes or is affected by them
- **Impact/Susceptibility**: Degree to which stakeholder is affected by project outcomes (positive or negative)
- **Legitimacy**: Appropriateness or validity of stakeholder's involvement or claims
- **Urgency**: Immediacy or time-sensitivity of stakeholder's needs, claims, or relationship to the project
- **Support/Opposition**: Current stance toward the project (strongly supportive, supportive, neutral, opposed, strongly opposed)
- **Knowledge/Expertise**: Understanding of relevant technical, business, or domain areas
- **Resources**: Ability to contribute funding, personnel, expertise, or other assets to the project
- **Network/Connections**: Relationships with other stakeholders that could amplify influence
- **Attitude/Disposition**: Emotional or psychological orientation toward the project or initiative
- **Expectations**: What stakeholder anticipates or hopes will happen as a result of the project
- **Dependencies**: Degree to which stakeholder relies on the project or its outcomes for their own success
- **Motivations**: Underlying reasons driving stakeholder's position or behavior
- **Issues/Concerns**: Specific topics, worries, or areas of focus for the stakeholder regarding the project

### Stakeholder Classification by Analysis Results

1. **Key Players**: High power, high interest - require close management and active engagement
2. **Context Setters**: High power, low interest - need to be kept satisfied to prevent them from becoming opposition
3. **Subjects**: Low power, high interest - need adequate information and consultation to prevent frustration
4. **Crowd**: Low power, low interest - require minimal effort but should be monitored for changes
5. **Advocates**: Actively support the project and can be leveraged as champions
6. **Opponents**: Actively resist or oppose the project and require mitigation strategies
7. **Swing Voters**: Could go either way depending on how the project evolves or how they're engaged
8. **Latent Stakeholders**: Have power or interest but not currently activated; could become relevant under certain conditions
9. **Defensive Stakeholders**: Primarily concerned with avoiding negative impacts rather than gaining benefits
10. **Opportunity-Seeking Stakeholders**: Primarily interested in gaining advantages or benefits from the project

### Analytical Techniques

1. **Scoring Models**: Assign numerical values to stakeholder attributes for comparative analysis
2. **Qualitative Assessment**: Use expert judgment to evaluate stakeholder characteristics descriptively
3. **Comparative Analysis**: Compare stakeholders against each other to identify relative positions
4. **Trend Analysis**: Track changes in stakeholder characteristics over time
5. **Gap Analysis**: Identify discrepancies between stakeholder expectations and project plans
6. **Conflict Analysis**: Identify areas where stakeholder interests or positions contradict each other
7. **Influence Mapping**: Trace how stakeholders affect each other and project decisions
8. **Interest Articulation**: Clarify what stakeholders actually want or need from the project
9. **Capacity Assessment**: Evaluate stakeholder ability to participate effectively in engagement processes
10. **Legitimacy Evaluation**: Assess the validity of stakeholder claims or involvement in the project context

## 7. Step-by-Step Process

1. **Review Stakeholder Register**: Ensure all identified stakeholders have sufficient baseline information for analysis
2. **Select Analysis Framework(s)**: Choose appropriate models based on project context and analysis objectives
3. **Gather Analytical Data**: Collect information needed for chosen analysis dimensions (interviews, documents, observation)
4. **Assess Power and Influence**: Evaluate each stakeholder's ability to affect project outcomes and decisions
5. **Evaluate Interest and Impact**: Determine how much stakeholders care about and are affected by the project
6. **Determine Legitimacy and Urgency**: Assess validity of stakeholder claims and time-sensitivity of their needs
7. **Gauge Attitude and Position**: Understand current stakeholder stance toward the project (support/oppose/neutral)
8. **Identify Interests and Concerns**: Clarify what specific aspects stakeholders care about or worry about
9. **Evaluate Resources and Capabilities**: Assess what stakeholders can contribute or how they might hinder progress
10. **Map Relationships and Networks**: Understand connections between stakeholders that could amplify influence
11. **Analyze Motivations and Drivers**: Understand underlying reasons for stakeholder positions or behaviors
12. **Assess Expectations and Anticipations**: Determine what stakeholders believe will or should happen
13. **Identify Potential Issues and Risks**: Recognize areas where stakeholder positions could create problems
14. **Determine Engagement Requirements**: Specify what type and level of interaction each stakeholder needs
15. **Prioritize Stakeholders**: Rank stakeholders based on analysis results to guide engagement focus
16. **Validate Analysis Findings**: Confirm accuracy with stakeholders themselves or knowledgeable informants
17. **Document Analytical Results**: Record assessments, scores, classifications, and insights for each stakeholder
18. **Develop Engagement Implications**: Translate analysis into specific engagement strategies and tactics
19. **Create Analytical Deliverables**: Produce matrices, maps, reports, and other outputs from the analysis
20. **Establish Update Mechanisms**: Define how stakeholder analysis will be refreshed as the project evolves
