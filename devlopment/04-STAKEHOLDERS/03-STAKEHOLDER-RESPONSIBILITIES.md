# 03 — STAKEHOLDER RESPONSIBILITIES

## 1. What Is This?

Stakeholder responsibilities refer to the specific duties, obligations, accountabilities, and expected behaviors that different stakeholder groups have in relation to a project, product, or initiative. This encompasses what stakeholders are expected to contribute, what decisions they need to make, what information they should provide, what approvals they must grant, and what actions they should take to support or enable the initiative's success. Understanding stakeholder responsibilities clarifies roles, prevents gaps or overlaps in accountability, and ensures effective collaboration across diverse stakeholder groups.

## 2. Why Does It Matter?

Understanding stakeholder responsibilities matters because:
- It prevents ambiguity about who should do what, reducing delays and confusion
- It ensures critical decisions are made by the appropriate authorities
- It clarifies what information stakeholders need to provide and when
- It establishes clear accountability for outcomes and deliverables
- It prevents duplication of effort or gaps in critical activities
- It enables stakeholders to prepare adequately for their roles
- It supports effective delegation and empowerment decisions
- It helps identify when stakeholders are not fulfilling their obligations
- It facilitates smoother handoffs between different project phases
- It reduces conflict arising from misunderstood or unmet expectations
- It supports compliance with governance requirements and regulations
- It enables better planning by accurately estimating stakeholder effort needs
- It creates a foundation for performance conversations and feedback
- It helps stakeholders prioritize their time and attention effectively

## 3. What Problem Does It Solve?

Without clarity on stakeholder responsibilities, teams commonly experience:
- Delays waiting for decisions that should have been made by specific stakeholders
- Rework when stakeholders provide input too late in the process
- Frustration when stakeholders feel their time is wasted on unnecessary involvement
- Critical information missing because no one was designated to provide it
- Approval bottlenecks when unclear who has authority to proceed
- Duplicated efforts when multiple stakeholders think they're responsible for the same task
- Important activities falling through the cracks when no one claims ownership
- Stakeholders overstepping boundaries and making decisions outside their authority
- Misunderstandings about what level of detail or format stakeholders need
- Inconsistent stakeholder participation leading to gaps in representation
- Blame-shifting when problems occur due to unclear accountability
- Ethical concerns when stakeholders make decisions without proper authority
- Legal risks from actions taken by unauthorized individuals
- Poor quality outcomes from inadequate stakeholder review or validation
- Regulatory compliance failures from missing required approvals

## 4. When Should We Use It?

Explicit definition of stakeholder responsibilities should be used:
- At the beginning of any project when forming the core team and governance structure
- When establishing steering committees, working groups, or advisory boards
- When defining RACI matrices (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed)
- When preparing stakeholder engagement or communication plans
- When defining roles for workshops, meetings, or other collaborative sessions
- When establishing decision-making processes and approval workflows
- When preparing contracts or agreements with external stakeholders
- When defining data sharing or information exchange protocols
- When establishing issue escalation and resolution procedures
- When preparing for organizational changes affecting roles and responsibilities
- When preparing for vendor or partner relationships requiring clear interfaces
- When defining citizen engagement processes for public initiatives
- When establishing community advisory boards or oversight committees
- When preparing regulatory submissions requiring documented stakeholder input

## 5. When Should We NOT Use It?

While defining stakeholder responsibilities is generally valuable, informal understanding might suffice when:
- Addressing urgent, safety, or security critical issues requiring immediate action
- Working on purely exploratory research with fixed, known participants
- Making minor, trivial changes with a stable, long-standing team
- The initiative has extremely short duration (hours or days) with clear leadership
- Well-established routines exist for the type of work being undertaken
- Emergency situations where delay for clarification could worsen outcomes
- Internal teams with long history of collaboration and implicit understanding
- Learning exercises with predefined roles in educational contexts
- When organizational policies already clearly define responsibilities for the initiative type
- When working with stakeholders who have standardized, well-understood roles
- When the scope is so limited that responsibility assumptions are obviously correct
- When stakeholders explicitly decline to take on any formal responsibilities

## 6. Core Concepts

### Responsibility Framework Types

1. **RACI Matrix**: Defines who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed for each activity or decision
2. **RAPID Model**: Clarifies who Recommends, Agrees, Performs, Inputs, and Decides
3. **DACI Framework**: Specifies Driver, Approver, Contributor, and Informed roles
4. **RASCI Model**: Extends RACI with Supportive role for those who assist the Responsible party
5. **CIARO Framework**: Outlines Consult, Input, Agree, Responsible, and Oversee roles
6. **Decision Rights Matrix**: Specifies who has authority for different types of decisions
7. **Information Flow Diagram**: Shows what information stakeholders should provide and receive
8. **Escalation Matrix**: Defines when and how issues should be elevated to higher authority
9. **Approval Workflow**: Documents the sequence of approvals required for different actions
10. **Service Level Agreement (SLA)**: Defines expected response times and quality standards between stakeholders

### Types of Stakeholder Responsibilities

1. **Decision-Making Responsibilities**: Authority to approve, reject, or make binding choices
2. **Information Provision Responsibilities**: Duty to supply data, expertise, or insights needed by others
3. **Resource Allocation Responsibilities**: Authority to commit budget, personnel, or equipment
4. **Review and Validation Responsibilities**: Obligation to examine work products for correctness or suitability
5. **Communication Responsibilities**: Duty to share information with specific audiences or groups
6. **Risk Management Responsibilities**: Obligation to identify, assess, or mitigate specific risks
7. **Compliance Responsibilities**: Duty to ensure adherence to laws, regulations, or standards
8. **Representational Responsibilities**: Obligation to represent and advocate for constituent interests
9. **Implementation Responsibilities**: Duty to execute specific tasks, activites, or deliverables
10. **Feedback Responsibilities**: Obligation to provide reactions, suggestions, or evaluations on work
11. **Resource Contribution Responsibilities**: Duty to provide funding, personnel, equipment, or other assets
12. **Coordination Responsibilities**: Obligation to align activities with other stakeholders or initiatives
13. **Champion/Advocacy Responsibilities**: Duty to promote support for the initiative among others
14. **Learning Responsibilities**: Obligation to capture and share lessons learned from participation
15. **Ethical Oversight Responsibilities**: Duty to ensure the initiative adheres to ethical standards

### Responsibility Characteristics

- **Authority Level**: Degree of decision-making power or sanctioning ability
- **Timeframe**: When the responsibility occurs (specific milestone, ongoing, event-triggered)
- **Frequency**: How often the responsibility needs to be exercised (one-time, periodic, continuous)
- **Scope**: What aspects of the project the responsibility covers (entire project, specific component)
- **Consequence**: What happens if the responsibility is not fulfilled (delay, increased risk, failure)
- **Interdependence**: Whether the responsibility relies on others fulfilling their duties first
- **Skill Requirements**: What knowledge, expertise, or capabilities are needed to fulfill the responsibility
- **Resource Requirements**: What time, budget, or other assets are needed to fulfill the responsibility
- **Accountability Mechanism**: How performance of the responsibility will be measured or evaluated
- **Delegation Potential**: Whether the responsibility can be transferred to another individual
- **Escalation Path**: What happens when issues arise that exceed the stakeholder's authority

### Common Responsibility Conflicts

1. **Authority Overlap**: Multiple stakeholders believe they have decision-making authority for the same matter
2. **Responsibility Gaps**: No stakeholder claims ownership for a critical activity or decision
3. **Conflicting Priorities**: Stakeholders have competing responsibilities that create tension
4. **Resource Contention**: Multiple stakeholders need the same limited resources at the same time
5. **Information Hoarding**: Stakeholders withhold information that others need to fulfill their responsibilities
6. **Role Ambiguity**: Unclear boundaries between what different stakeholders should do
7. **Accountability Diffusion**: No clear owner when problems occur, leading to blame-shifting
8. **Expectation Mismatch**: Different understandings of what constitutes fulfilling a responsibility
9. **Capacity Mismatch**: Stakeholders lack time, expertise, or resources to fulfill assigned responsibilities
10. **Motivation Misalignment**: Stakeholders lack incentive to fulfill their responsibilities effectively

## 7. Step-by-Step Process

1. **Identify Key Activities and Decisions**: List all major tasks, deliverables, and decision points in the initiative
2. **Map Activities to Stakeholders**: Determine which stakeholders are involved in each activity or decision
3. **Determine Responsibility Types**: For each stakeholder-activity pair, specify what type of responsibility applies
4. **Establish Authority Levels**: Define what decisions each stakeholder can make independently vs. requiring escalation
5. **Specify Information Requirements**: Determine what information each stakeholder needs to provide or receive
6. **Define Timing and Triggers**: Establish when each responsibility should be exercised (specific dates, events)
7. **Clarify Standards and Criteria**: Define what constitutes satisfactory fulfillment of each responsibility
8. **Identify Dependencies**: Map which responsibilities must be completed before others can begin
9. **Document Escalation Paths**: Define what happens when stakeholders encounter issues beyond their authority
10. **Validate with Stakeholders**: Review the responsibility assignments with stakeholders themselves for accuracy
11. **Create Responsibility Matrix**: Compile all assignments into a clear, accessible format (RACI, etc.)
12. **Communicate Responsibilities**: Share the responsibility definitions with all stakeholders
13. **Establish Monitoring Mechanisms**: Define how responsibility fulfillment will be tracked and reported
14. **Schedule Regular Reviews**: Set intervals to review and update responsibilities as the initiative evolves
15. **Address Gaps and Conflicts**: Resolve any identified omissions, overlaps, or disagreements in assignments
16. **Integrate with Governance**: Align responsibility definitions with overall project governance structure
17. **Document for Reference**: Create accessible reference materials for stakeholders to consult
18. **Establish Consequences**: Define what happens when responsibilities are consistently fulfilled or neglected
19. **Create Onboarding Materials**: Develop resources to help new stakeholders understand their responsibilities
20. **Plan for Transition**: Define how responsibilities will change or end as the initiative concludes
