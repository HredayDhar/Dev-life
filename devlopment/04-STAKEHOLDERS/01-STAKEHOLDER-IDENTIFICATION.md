# 01 — STAKEHOLDER IDENTIFICATION

## 1. What Is This?

Stakeholder identification is the process of systematically discovering and documenting all individuals, groups, or organizations that may affect or be affected by a project, product, or initiative. This foundational step in stakeholder analysis involves creating a comprehensive list of potential stakeholders before assessing their interests, influence, or importance. Effective stakeholder identification ensures that no relevant parties are overlooked, reducing the risk of missed requirements, unexpected resistance, or project failure due to unidentified constituencies.

## 2. Why Does It Matter?

Stakeholder identification matters because:
- It forms the foundation for all subsequent stakeholder analysis and engagement activities
- It prevents costly oversights by ensuring all relevant parties are considered from the start
- It helps uncover hidden stakeholders who may have significant influence despite low visibility
- It enables comprehensive risk assessment by identifying all potential sources of support or opposition
- It supports inclusive decision-making by ensuring diverse perspectives are represented
- It facilitates early engagement strategies that can build buy-in and reduce resistance
- It ensures compliance with regulations that may require consultation with specific stakeholder groups
- It helps identify potential champions and advocates who can support the initiative
- It prevents embarrassing situations where key stakeholders feel excluded or overlooked
- It supports ethical practice by ensuring affected parties have opportunity to provide input
- It enables better resource allocation by focusing engagement efforts where they're most needed
- It creates a baseline for tracking stakeholder attitudes and engagement effectiveness over time

## 3. What Problem Does It Solve?

Without thorough stakeholder identification, teams commonly experience:
- Building solutions that fail to address critical needs of overlooked user groups
- Missing regulatory requirements due to unidentified compliance stakeholders
- Unexpected opposition from stakeholders who felt excluded from the process
- Rework when newly discovered stakeholders introduce conflicting requirements
- Delayed timelines due to belated discovery of approval requirements
- Budget overruns from emergency stakeholder management activities
- Reputational damage from appearing insensitive to affected communities
- Ethical concerns from making decisions that impact people without their input
- Strategic blind spots from missing perspectives that could inform better solutions
- Communication breakdowns when key information channels are overlooked
- Weak business cases that fail to address all relevant stakeholder concerns
- Poor adoption because solutions don't work for unidentified user segments
- Legal challenges from stakeholders who claim they should have been consulted

## 4. When Should We Use It?

Stakeholder identification should be used:
- At the very beginning of any project, before detailed planning commences
- When entering new markets or launching new products/services
- When preparing for organizational changes that affect multiple groups
- When responding to stakeholder complaints about feeling excluded
- When planning major technology implementations with broad impact
- When initiating vendor relationships that may affect internal teams
- When preparing for regulatory submissions requiring stakeholder consultation
- When planning mergers, acquisitions, or significant structural changes
- When developing corporate social responsibility initiatives
- When preparing investment proposals requiring demonstration of broad support
- When conducting competitive analysis that affects multiple constituencies
- When planning organizational restructuring that impacts employees
- When preparing for crisis management scenarios requiring broad coordination

## 5. When Should We NOT Use It?

While stakeholder identification is generally valuable, minimal formal identification might be appropriate when:
- Addressing urgent, safety, or security critical issues requiring immediate action (though even then, rapid identification should occur)
- Working on purely exploratory research with no external impact or data collection
- Making minor, trivial changes with no user effect or external visibility
- The stakeholder landscape is extremely stable, well-documented, and unchanged from recent similar projects
- In maintenance contexts where the goal is simply system stability with no functional changes
- Emergency situations where delay for comprehensive identification could worsen outcomes
- Well-established internal tools with fixed, known user bases and unchanged requirements
- Learning exercises or proof-of-concepts with no expectation of production use or external impact
- When working on infrastructure improvements with clear, direct, and limited stakeholders
- When the request comes from a single, clearly identified stakeholder with limited scope
- When organizational policies explicitly define stakeholder groups for certain types of initiatives

## 6. Core Concepts

### Stakeholder Typology Frameworks

1. **Primary vs Secondary Stakeholders**: Primary stakeholders are directly affected by or have direct influence on the project; secondary stakeholders are indirectly affected or have indirect influence.

2. **Internal vs External Stakeholders**: Internal stakeholders are within the organization (employees, management, shareholders); external stakeholders are outside (customers, regulators, community, suppliers).

3. **Beneficiaries vs Adversaries**: Beneficiaries gain positive outcomes from the project; adversaries may experience negative consequences or oppose the project.

4. **Decision Makers vs Influencers vs Implementers**: Decision makers have authority to approve or reject; influencers affect opinions of others; implementers execute the work.

5. **Supporters vs Neutrals vs Opponents**: Supporters actively favor the initiative; neutrals have no strong position; opponents actively resist or oppose.

6. **Formal vs Informal Stakeholders**: Formal stakeholders have official roles or positions; informal stakeholders influence through social networks or expertise.

7. **Voluntary vs Involuntary Stakeholders**: Voluntary stakeholders choose to engage; involuntary stakeholders are affected regardless of their choice.

### Identification Techniques

1. **Brainstorming**: Structured sessions with project team and subject matter experts to list potential stakeholders
2. **Document Analysis**: Reviewing organizational charts, reports, contracts, and historical data
3. **Interviewing**: Speaking with knowledgeable individuals to identify stakeholders they know
4. **Snowball Sampling**: Asking identified stakeholders to recommend others who should be included
5. **Stakeholder Mapping**: Visual techniques to identify relationships and gaps in stakeholder coverage
6. ** Categoria1ization Frameworks**: Using predefined categories (users, regulators, suppliers, etc.) to ensure completeness
7. **Impact Analysis**: Identifying who might be affected by potential project outcomes
8. **Influence Analysis**: Identifying who might be able to affect project success or failure
9. **Expert Judgment**: Leveraging experience from similar projects or domains
10. **Compliance Checklists**: Using regulatory or industry standards that specify required consultations

### Stakeholder Characteristics to Document

- **Name/Identifier**: How to refer to the stakeholder (individual name, group name, organization)
- **Type/Category**: Classification according to chosen framework (internal/external, primary/secondary, etc.)
- **Contact Information**: How to reach the stakeholder (email, phone, address, preferred method)
- **Representative**: Specific person who speaks for the stakeholder group (if applicable)
- **Mission/Objectives**: What the stakeholder aims to achieve or protect
- **Interests/Concerns**: What the stakeholder cares about regarding the project
- **Expectations**: What the stakeholder anticipates or hopes will happen
- **Potential Impact**: How the project could affect the stakeholder (positive, negative, neutral)
- **Influence/Power**: Ability to affect project outcomes or decisions
- **Interest/Concern Level**: Degree to which stakeholder cares about project outcomes
- **Attitude/Disposition**: Current stance toward the project (supportive, neutral, opposed)
- **Knowledge/Expertise**: Understanding of relevant domains or technical areas
- **Resources**: Ability to contribute funding, personnel, expertise, or other assets
- **Network/Connections**: Relationships with other stakeholders
- **Communication Preferences**: How the stakeholder prefers to receive information
- **Availability/Accessibility**: Ease of engaging with the stakeholder
- **Legal Status**: Any special legal standing or rights the stakeholder possesses
- **Historical Relationship**: Past interactions with the organization or similar projects

### Sources for Stakeholder Identification

- **Organizational Documentation**: Org charts, directories, role descriptions, responsibility matrices
- **Project Documentation**: Business cases, project charters, feasibility studies, prior project reports
- **External Databases**: Government registries, industry directories, professional associations
- **Market Research**: Customer surveys, focus group transcripts, demographic data
- **Legal/Regulatory Sources**: Laws, regulations, compliance requirements, advisory committee lists
- **Financial Records**: Shareholder lists, investor reports, beneficiary records, funding source documentation
- **Operational Records**: Customer databases, supplier lists, partner agreements, service logs
- **Social Media Analysis**: Online communities, discussion forums, influencer identification
- **News Media Coverage**: Press releases, news articles, public statements, expert commentary
- **Community Resources**: Neighborhood associations, advocacy groups, local organization listings
- **Academic Sources**: Research papers, expert directories, university department listings
- **Professional Networks**: Conference attendee lists, professional association memberships
- **Subject Matter Experts**: Internal employees, domain specialists, experienced practitioners
- **Historical Data**: Previous stakeholder registers, lesson learned reports, audit findings

## 7. Step-by-Step Process

1. **Clarify Project Scope and Objectives**: Define what the initiative entails and what boundaries apply
2. **Review Existing Documentation**: Gather organizational charts, project documents, historical stakeholder lists
3. **Conduct Initial Brainstorming Session**: Engage project team to list all potential stakeholders they can think of
4. **Categorize Initial List**: Group stakeholders by type (internal/external, primary/secondary, etc.) to identify gaps
5. **Identify Key Informants**: Determine who has broad knowledge of the stakeholder landscape
6. **Conduct Stakeholder Interviews**: Speak with key informants to expand and validate the stakeholder list
7. **Review Regulatory Requirements**: Check for legally mandated stakeholder consultations or notifications
8. **Analyze Similar Projects**: Review stakeholder lists from comparable initiatives for additional candidates
9. **Apply Categorization Frameworks**: Use predefined categories (users, regulators, suppliers, etc.) to ensure completeness
10. **Perform Snowball Sampling**: Ask newly identified stakeholders to recommend others who should be included
11. **Review Public Sources**: Examine news reports, social media, community listings for additional stakeholders
12. **Validate Completeness**: Use techniques like gap analysis or coverage checks to assess thoroughness
13. **Document Stakeholder Details**: Record contact information, representative, interests, and other relevant attributes
14. **Create Stakeholder Register**: Compile all identified stakeholders into a single, accessible reference
15. **Review and Refine**: Have project sponsor or leadership review the list for completeness and accuracy
16. **Establish Maintenance Process**: Define how the stakeholder list will be updated as the project evolves
