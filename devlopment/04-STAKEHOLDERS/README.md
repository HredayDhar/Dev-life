# 04 — STAKEHOLDERS

## 1. What Is This?

Stakeholder analysis is the systematic identification, assessment, and prioritization of individuals, groups, or organizations that can affect or be affected by a project, product, or initiative. It involves understanding their interests, influence, expectations, and potential impact on success. In software engineering, stakeholder analysis ensures that the right people are engaged at the right time with the right information to build solutions that truly meet needs and gain acceptance.

## 2. Why Does It Matter?

Stakeholder analysis matters because:
- It prevents building elegant technology that nobody wants or needs
- It identifies sources of support and resistance early in the process
- It ensures critical requirements aren't missed from key stakeholder groups
- It helps manage expectations and reduce surprises during development
- It increases the likelihood of adoption and user satisfaction
- It enables effective communication planning and engagement strategies
- It identifies potential conflicts and allows for proactive resolution
- It ensures regulatory and compliance stakeholders are properly engaged
- It helps prioritize efforts when resources are limited
- It builds relationships that can support future initiatives
- It reduces project risk from unidentified or misunderstood stakeholders
- It improves decision-making by incorporating diverse perspectives

## 3. What Problem Does It Solve?

Without proper stakeholder analysis, teams commonly experience:
- Building solutions that fail to address real user needs or business problems
- Missing critical requirements from influential stakeholder groups
- Unexpected resistance or opposition during implementation
- Poor adoption due to lack of buy-in from key constituencies
- Wasted effort on features that stakeholders don't value or need
- Rework due to misunderstood or conflicting requirements
- Delays from unidentified approval processes or stakeholders
- Compliance issues from missing regulatory stakeholders
- Reputational damage from appearing insensitive to stakeholder concerns
- Increased project costs from poor stakeholder management
- Failure to identify and leverage potential champions and advocates
- Communication breakdowns leading to misunderstandings and errors
- Ethical concerns from excluding affected parties from decisions

## 4. When Should We Use It?

Stakeholder analysis should be used:
- At the beginning of any project, product, or initiative
- When entering new markets or launching new products/services
- When preparing for significant organizational changes
- When responding to stakeholder complaints or concerns
- When planning major technology implementations or upgrades
- When initiating vendor or partnership relationships
- When preparing for regulatory audits or compliance reviews
- When planning mergers, acquisitions, or divestitures
- When developing community engagement or public relations strategies
- When preparing investment proposals or funding requests
- When conducting competitive analysis or market research
- When planning organizational restructuring
- When preparing for crisis management or emergency response

## 5. When Should We NOT Use It?

While stakeholder analysis is generally valuable, minimal formal analysis might be appropriate when:
- Addressing urgent, safety, or security critical issues requiring immediate action
- Working on purely exploratory research or technical spikes with no external impact
- Making minor, trivial UI tweaks or cosmetic changes with no user effect
- The stakeholder landscape is stable, well-understood, and not changing
- In maintenance contexts where the goal is simply system stability with no changes
- Emergency situations where delay for analysis could worsen outcomes
- Well-established systems where stakeholder relationships are crystal clear
- Internal tooling with a fixed, known user base and unchanged requirements
- Learning exercises or proof-of-concepts with no expectation of production use
- When working on infrastructure improvements with clear, direct stakeholders
- When the request comes from a single, clearly identified stakeholder

## 6. Core Concepts

### Stakeholder Classification Models

1. **Power/Interest Grid**: Classifies stakeholders by their power to influence and interest in the project
2. **Influence/Impact Grid**: Assesses stakeholders by their ability to influence outcomes and degree to which they're affected
3. **Salience Model**: Considers power, legitimacy, and urgency to determine stakeholder prominence
4. **Radial Diagram**: Visualizes stakeholders in concentric circles based on proximity to the project
5. **Categorization by Type**: Groups stakeholders by role (users, executives, regulators, etc.)
6. **Stakeholder Cube**: Three-dimensional model using power, interest, and attitude
7. **Force Field Analysis**: Identifies driving and restraining forces for change
8. **Mitchell, Agle, and Wood's Model**: Based on power, legitimacy, and urgency

### Stakeholder Attributes

- **Power/Authority**: Ability to influence project outcomes or decisions
- **Interest/Concern**: Degree to which stakeholder cares about project outcomes
- **Influence**: Capacity to affect other stakeholders or project processes
- **Legitimacy**: Appropriateness of stakeholder's involvement
- **Urgency**: Immediacy of stakeholder's claims or needs
- **Attitude/Support**: Level of support or opposition to the project
- **Knowledge/Expertise**: Understanding of relevant domains or contexts
- **Resources**: Ability to contribute funding, personnel, or other assets
- **Network/Connections**: Relationships with other stakeholders
- **Dependency**: Degree to which stakeholder relies on project outcomes

### Engagement Levels

1. **Unaware**: Stakeholder doesn't know about the project or issue
2. **Resistant**: Stakeholder knows about project and opposes it
3. **Neutral**: Stakeholder knows about project but neither supports nor opposes
4. **Supportive**: Stakeholder knows about project and supports it
5. **Leading**: Stakeholder actively champions and promotes the project

### Communication Modalities

- **Informational**: One-way communication providing updates or facts
- **Consultative**: Two-way communication seeking input or feedback
- **Involvement**: Stakeholder participation in workshops or working groups
- **Collaboration**: Joint decision-making and problem-solving
- **Empowerment**: Delegating decision-making authority to stakeholders

### Stakeholder Lifecycle

1. **Identification**: Discovering who the stakeholders are
2. **Analysis**: Understanding their characteristics and interests
3. **Prioritization**: Determining which stakeholders need most attention
4. **Engagement Planning**: Developing strategies for interaction
5. **Engagement Execution**: Implementing communication and involvement activities
6. **Monitoring**: Tracking stakeholder attitudes and engagement effectiveness
7. **Adaptation**: Adjusting strategies based on feedback and changing conditions

## 7. Step-by-Step Process

1. **Define Analysis Purpose**: Determine why stakeholder analysis is needed and how it will be used
2. **Identify Potential Stakeholders**: Brainstorm all possible individuals, groups, or organizations
3. **Gather Stakeholder Information**: Collect data on interests, influence, expectations, etc.
4. **Analyze Stakeholder Characteristics**: Assess power, interest, attitude, and other attributes
5. **Map Stakeholder Relationships**: Understand connections, alliances, and conflicts between stakeholders
6. **Prioritize Stakeholders**: Determine which stakeholders require focused attention
7. **Develop Engagement Strategies**: Plan how to interact with each stakeholder group
8. **Create Communication Plans**: Define what, when, how, and to whom to communicate
9. **Implement Engagement Activities**: Execute workshops, interviews, briefings, etc.
10. **Monitor Stakeholder Responses**: Track feedback, attitudes, and engagement effectiveness
11. **Update Analysis Regularly**: Refresh stakeholder understanding as conditions change
12. **Document Lessons Learned**: Capture insights for future stakeholder initiatives

Inputs to stakeholder analysis include:
- Organizational charts and reporting structures
- Project charters, proposals, and business cases
- Market research and customer data
- Regulatory and compliance requirement documents
- Vendor and partner contracts
- Community organization listings and membership directories
- Social media analysis and online community identification
- Previous project stakeholder registers and lessons learned
- Public records, filings, and regulatory databases
- News media coverage and public statements
- Employee directories and role descriptions
- Professional association membership lists
- Academic and research institution contacts
- NGO and advocacy organization registries
- Government agency contacts and department structures
- Shareholder and investor information
- Union and employee representative contacts
- Customer databases and CRM systems
- Support ticket and complaint histories
- Survey results and feedback collections
- Event attendee lists and registration data
- Expert panels and advisory board memberships
- Lobbying records and political contribution disclosures

## 9. Outputs / Deliverables

Outputs from stakeholder analysis include:
- Stakeholder register or database with contact information
- Stakeholder analysis matrix (power/interest, influence/impact, etc.)
- Stakeholder mapping visualizations (radial diagrams, network graphs, etc.)
- Engagement strategy documents for different stakeholder groups
- Communication plans with timing, channels, and responsibilities
- Issue logs tracking stakeholder concerns and responses
- Commitment registers documenting stakeholder pledges and support
- Risk registers identifying stakeholder-related threats and opportunities
- Change readiness assessments for different stakeholder groups
- Training and development plans for stakeholder capacity building
- Feedback mechanisms and reporting structures
- Meeting schedules and facilitation guides
- Stakeholder satisfaction measurement approaches
- Issue escalation procedures and conflict resolution processes
- Stakeholder-specific training materials and briefings
- Reporting dashboards for tracking stakeholder engagement
- Documentation of lessons learned and best practices
- Stakeholder management plans integrated with overall project plans

## 10. Real-World Example

**Context**: A city implementing a new smart traffic management system.

**Stakeholder Analysis Process**:
- Reviewed city organizational charts and department responsibilities
- Analyzed traffic accident reports, congestion data, and public transit usage
- Identified relevant municipal departments (transportation, public works, police, emergency services)
- Examined state and federal transportation regulations and funding requirements
- Reviewed previous traffic improvement projects and public feedback
- Analyzed local business associations, neighborhood groups, and advocacy organizations
- Studied commuting patterns, demographic data, and land use plans
- Identified technology vendors, consultants, and potential system integrators
- Examined privacy advocacy groups and civil liberties organizations
- Reviewed accessibility organizations and disability rights groups
- Analyzed emergency response times and evacuation route requirements
- Studied environmental impact reports and sustainability goals
- Identified media outlets and communication channels serving the community
- Reviewed university research centers and transportation studies programs

**Stakeholder Analysis Results**:

**Primary Stakeholders (High Power, High Interest)**:
- **City Transportation Department**: Responsible for traffic management, signal timing, and road maintenance
- **Emergency Services (Police, Fire, EMS)**: Require reliable traffic flow for response times and evacuation routes
- **Public Transit Agency**: Operates buses and needs signal priority for schedule adherence
- **Mayor's Office**: Sets priorities and allocates funding for city initiatives
- **City Council**: Approves budgets and oversees department performance
- **Major Employers**: Concerned about employee commute times and commercial access
- **Downtown Business Association**: Represents retail and hospitality businesses affected by traffic patterns
- **Regional Transportation Authority**: Coordinates multi-jurisdictional transportation planning
- **State Department of Transportation**: Provides funding and sets design standards for roadways
- **Federal Highway Administration**: Oversees compliance with federal transportation regulations

**Secondary Stakeholders (Moderate Power, Moderate Interest)**:
- **Neighborhood Associations**: Represent residents concerned about noise, pollution, and access
- **Environmental Groups**: Focus on emissions reduction, sustainable transportation, and air quality
- **Disability Advocacy Organizations**: Ensure accessibility for people with mobility, vision, or hearing impairments
- **Cycling and Pedestrian Advocacy Groups**: Promote active transportation and safe street design
- **Ride-sharing Companies**: Affected by changes to traffic patterns and pickup/drop-off zones
- **Delivery and Logistics Companies**: Concerned about commercial vehicle access and delivery times
- **Parking Facility Operators**: Impacted by changes to traffic flow and parking demand patterns
- **Local Media Outlets**: Report on traffic conditions and public infrastructure projects
- **School Districts and Universities**: Concerned about student transportation and campus access
- **Hospitals and Healthcare Facilities**: Require reliable access for patients, staff, and emergency vehicles
- **Insurance Companies**: Interested in accident rates and claims data related to traffic patterns
- **Automotive Clubs and Associations**: Represent driver interests and provide public input

**Tertiary Stakeholders (Low Power, Variable Interest)**:
- **Individual Commuters**: Daily travelers affected by traffic conditions and travel times
- **Tourism and Hospitality Industry**: Concerned about visitor access and experience
- **Real Estate Developers**: Interested in how traffic affects property values and development potential
- **Taxi and Limousine Services**: Affected by changes to urban mobility patterns

- **Bicycle and Pedestrian Injury Advocacy Groups**: Focus on safety improvements for vulnerable road users

- Examined regulatory requirements from banking regulators, consumer protection agencies, and data privacy authorities
- Reviewed previous IT implementation projects and post-implementation reviews
- Analyzed vendor contracts, service level agreements, and technology partnership agreements
- Studied competitor CRM implementations and industry analyst reports
- Identified technology vendors, consultants, and potential system integrators
- Examined employee unions, professional associations, and employee resource groups
- Reviewed audit reports, internal control documentation, and risk assessments
- Studied marketing campaigns, customer satisfaction surveys, and complaint handling procedures
- Analyzed data governance policies, information security requirements, and business continuity plans
- Identified shareholder groups, investor relations contacts, and financial analysts
