# 02 — WHO HAS THE PROBLEM

## 1. What Is This?

"Who has the problem" refers to the identification and understanding of all stakeholders, users, and entities affected by or concerned with a specific problem. This goes beyond just identifying end-users to include anyone who experiences the problem directly, indirectly, or has a stake in its resolution. Understanding who has the problem is crucial for ensuring that solutions address the needs of all affected parties and don't inadvertently create new problems for others.

## 2. Why Does It Matter?

Accurately identifying who has the problem matters because:
- Solutions designed for the wrong users fail to achieve adoption
- Overlooking stakeholder groups leads to unexpected resistance or workarounds
- Different user segments may experience the same problem differently
- Focusing only on vocal minorities can neglect silent majority needs
- Stakeholders have different priorities, constraints, and definitions of success
- Ignoring certain stakeholders can create new problems or shift burdens
- Understanding the full ecosystem prevents unintended consequences
- Successful solutions require buy-in from all affected parties
- Different stakeholders may provide complementary insights about the problem

## 3. What Problem Does It Solve?

Without properly identifying who has the problem, teams commonly experience:
- Building solutions that work for some users but create problems for others
- Low adoption rates because the solution doesn't address core user needs
- Unexpected resistance from stakeholders who weren't consulted
- Solutions that shift the problem from one group to another rather than solving it
- Missed opportunities to solve related problems for connected stakeholders
- Wasted effort on features that don't matter to the actual users
- Communication breakdowns between teams with different stakeholder perspectives
- Failure to anticipate implementation or operational challenges
- Solutions that create compliance, security, or privacy issues for overlooked stakeholders

## 4. When Should We Use It?

Stakeholder and user identification should occur:
- At the beginning of problem definition activities
- Before conducting user research or interviews
- When segmenting users for targeted research or solution design
- When trying to understand varying impacts of a problem across groups
- Before making decisions that will affect different user segments differently
- When planning communication or change management strategies
- During ongoing solution development to validate assumptions
- When preparing for deployment or rollout to different user groups
- When measuring success to ensure all affected parties benefit

## 5. When Should We NOT Use It?

While stakeholder analysis is generally valuable, minimal formal identification might be appropriate when:
- Addressing extremely well-understood problems with stable, known user bases
- Working on technical infrastructure problems where users are primarily internal systems
- Dealing with urgent, time-critical issues where rapid response is more valuable than comprehensive analysis
- The problem and affected parties are self-evident and uncontested
- Making minor, low-risk improvements to well-established features
- Internal tooling where the user base is clearly defined and stable
- Problems that affect only a single, homogenous user group with clear characteristics

## 6. Core Concepts

### Types of Stakeholders and Affected Parties

1. **Primary Users**: People who directly interact with the system and experience the problem firsthand
2. **Secondary Users**: People who are affected by the problem through their interaction with primary users
3. **Internal Stakeholders**: Employees, teams, or departments within the organization affected by the problem
4. **External Stakeholders**: Customers, partners, regulators, or community members outside the organization
5. **Decision Makers**: People who control resources, prioritize work, or make go/no-go decisions
6. **Implementation Partners**: Teams or vendors who will build, deploy, or maintain the solution
7. **Opponents or Critics**: People who may resist changes or have competing interests
8. **Subject Matter Experts**: People with specialized knowledge about the problem domain

### Stakeholder Characteristics to Understand

- **Goals and Objectives**: What each stakeholder hopes to achieve
- **Pain Points**: Specific ways the problem affects them negatively
- **Current Workarounds**: How they currently deal with the problem
- **Influence and Authority**: Their ability to affect decisions or block progress
- **Urgency**: How urgently they need the problem solved
- **Technical Proficiency**: Their comfort level with technology and complexity
- **Access Constraints**: Limitations on when, where, or how they can interact with systems
- **Communication Preferences**: How they prefer to receive information and provide feedback
- **Success Criteria**: How they would judge whether the problem has been solved
- **Risks and Concerns**: What they worry might happen during problem-solving

### Stakeholder Mapping Techniques

1. **Power/Interest Grid**: Plotting stakeholders by their power to influence and interest in the problem
2. **Influence/Impact Matrix**: Understanding who affects the problem and who is affected by it
3. **User Journey Mapping**: Identifying touchpoints where different stakeholders encounter the problem
4. **Stakeholder Salience Model**: Considering power, legitimacy, and urgency attributes
5. **RACI Matrix**: Defining who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed
6. **Empathy Mapping**: Understanding what stakeholders think, feel, say, and do

## 7. Step-by-Step Process

1. **Start with the Obvious**: Begin with the users or systems mentioned in the initial problem description
2. **Brainstorm Broadly**: List all possible individuals, groups, or systems that might be affected
3. **Categorize Stakeholders**: Group stakeholders by type (users, internal, external, etc.)
4. **Research and Validate**: Talk to initial stakeholders to identify others you may have missed
5. **Create Stakeholder Profiles**: Document key characteristics for each stakeholder group
6. **Map Relationships**: Understand how different stakeholders interact and influence each other
7. **Identify Extremes**: Look for edge case users or stakeholders with unique needs
8. **Assess Influence and Interest**: Determine which stakeholders have most to gain/lose and most influence
9. **Plan Engagement**: Determine how to involve different stakeholders in the problem-solving process
10. **Document and Share**: Create a stakeholder register or map to share with the team
11. **Review and Update**: Regularly revisit stakeholder understanding as new information emerges

## 8. Inputs

Inputs to identifying who has the problem include:
- Initial problem description or complaint
- Organizational charts or team structures
- Customer or user databases and segmentation data
- Support ticket or complaint logs showing who reports issues
- Usage analytics showing who interacts with relevant systems
- Sales or account management information about customer segments
- Regulatory or compliance requirements affecting certain groups
- Previous stakeholder analyses from related projects
- Market research or user persona documentation
- Subject matter expert knowledge about the domain
- Social media or community discussions about related topics
- Supplier or partner information about external entities
- Employee feedback or internal communication channels

## 9. Outputs / Deliverables

Outputs from stakeholder identification include:
- Stakeholder register or database listing all identified parties
- Stakeholder maps or visualizations showing relationships and influence
- User personas or profiles for primary user segments
- Stakeholder analysis reports detailing characteristics and interests
- Communication plans tailored to different stakeholder groups
- Engagement strategies for involving stakeholders in the process
- Lists of stakeholder goals, pain points, and success criteria
- Identification of potential conflicts or competing interests between stakeholders
- Gaps in understanding that require further research
- Recommendations for stakeholder management approaches

## 10. Real-World Example

**Context**: A hospital system implementing a new electronic prescription refill process.

**Initial Assumption**: "Patients who need prescription refills have the problem."

**Stakeholder Identification Process**:
- Interviewed patients about refill experiences
- Spoke with pharmacists about workflow challenges
- Consulted with doctors about prescription management
- Interviewed insurance specialists about authorization processes
- Talked to IT staff about system integration challenges
- Consulted with hospital administrators about regulatory requirements
- Spoke with family members who assist elderly patients
- Contacted pharmacy benefit managers about reimbursement rules

**Stakeholder Identification Results**:

**Primary Users (Experience Problem Directly)**:
- Patients needing prescription refills (varied by age, tech literacy, condition)
- Clinic staff who initiate refill requests
- Pharmacy technicians who process refills
- Pharmacists who verify and authorize refills

**Secondary Users (Indirectly Affected)**:
- Family members or caregivers assisting patients with refills
- Physicians' office staff managing patient communications
- Insurance company staff processing authorization requests
- Pharmacy benefit managers setting formulary and reimbursement rules

**Internal Stakeholders (Within Hospital System)**:
- IT department responsible for system implementation and maintenance
- Hospital administrators concerned with compliance and costs
- Quality assurance teams monitoring patient safety
- Training staff responsible for user education
- Security team ensuring HIPAA compliance
- Finance department tracking costs and reimbursement

**External Stakeholders (Outside Hospital System)**:
- Regulatory bodies (FDA, DEA for controlled substances)
- State pharmacy boards setting practice requirements
- Electronic health record vendors providing underlying systems
- Pharmacy suppliers affected by changes in refill patterns
- Patient advocacy groups concerned with access to medication

**Key Insights from Stakeholder Analysis**:
- Elderly patients often rely on family members to manage refills
- Physicians' offices frequently initiate refills on patients' behalf
- Insurance prior authorization creates delays patients don't control
- Pharmacy technicians bear brunt of patient frustration about delays
- Different controlled substances have varying regulatory requirements
- Hospital administrators need audit trails for compliance reporting
- IT staff must balance security requirements with usability needs
- Family members often need proxy access to manage refills for relatives

**Consequences of Missing Stakeholders**:
- Initial designs focused only on patient-facing interface neglected physician office workflow
- System didn't support proxy access needed by elderly patients and caregivers
- Lack of考虑 for insurance authorization timing caused confusion
- No provision for pharmacist override capabilities created workflow bottlenecks
- Missing audit Trail features would have caused compliance failures

## 11. Technical Example

**Context**: A financial trading platform experiencing latency issues during market open.

**Initial Assumption**: "Traders using the platform have the latency problem."

**Stakeholder Identification Process**:
- Interviewed active traders about their experience during market open
- Consulted with platform engineers about system architecture
- Spoke with risk management about monitoring requirements
- Talked to compliance officers about regulatory obligations
- Interviewed clearing houses about settlement dependencies
- Consulted with market data providers about feed timing
- Spoke with customer support about user complaints
- Consulted with business development about client requirements
- Talked to infrastructure team about hosting and network arrangements
- Interviewed quantitative analysts about strategy performance impacts

**Stakeholder Identification Results**:

**Primary Users (Directly Experience Latency)**:
- Retail traders placing trades during market open
- Institutional traders executing large orders at market open
- Market makers providing liquidity during volatile periods
- Algorithmic traders running time-sensitive strategies
- Day traders relying on rapid execution for scalping strategies

**Secondary Users (Indirectly Affected)**:
- Portfolio managers whose strategies depend on timely execution
- Compliance officers monitoring for best execution violations
- Clearing houses processing trades with potentially stale prices
- Regulators monitoring market fairness and stability
- Technology vendors providing co-location or connectivity services
- Exchange operators whose rules assume certain latency characteristics

**Internal Stakeholders (Within Trading Firm)**:
- Trading desk heads responsible for profitability and risk
- quantitative analysts developing latency-sensitive strategies
- Risk management monitoring execution quality and slippage
- Compliance team ensuring regulatory adherence (best execution, etc.)
- Technology operations maintaining platform performance
- Development team building and deploying platform updates
- Business development team acquiring and retaining clients
- Customer support handling user complaints about performance
- Finance team measuring impact on profitability and costs
- Legal team assessing liability from execution failures

**External Stakeholders (Outside Trading Firm)**:
- Exchange operators setting listing and trading rules
- Regulatory bodies (SEC, CFTC, FCA) overseeing market operations
- Clearing houses (DTCC, OCC) managing settlement and risk
- Market data providers supplying real-time prices and feeds
- Liquidity providers whose strategies interact with latency effects
- Arbitrageurs exploiting price differences across venues
- News and research providers timing releases around market events
- Hardware vendors supplying specialized trading equipment
- Telecommunications providers offering network connectivity

**Key Insights from Stakeholder Analysis**:
- Different trading strategies have vastly different latency sensitivities
- Retail traders care more about reliability than microsecond latency
- Institutional traders need consistent latency, not just speed
- Compliance officers need audit trails showing execution timing
- Clearing houses are affected by the quality of prices at execution time
- Market data latency contributes to perceived platform latency
- Different order types (market, limit, stop) have different latency requirements
- Risk management needs real-time monitoring, not just historical data
- Customer support needs to distinguish between platform issues and user error
- Business development must balance performance promises with technical reality
- Infrastructure decisions affect geographic distribution of users differently

**Consequences of Missing Stakeholders**:
- Initial optimization focused only on reducing average latency neglected worst-case scenarios important to risk management
- System didn't provide the execution timing details needed for compliance reporting
- Market data feeds weren't optimized, creating bottleneck before platform even received data
- Alerting system didn't notify risk managers of developing latency issues in time
- Training materials didn't help users distinguish between different types of latency effects
- Pricing model didn't account for the varying value of latency improvements to different user segments
- International users experienced different latency characteristics due to routing decisions not considered

## 12. Good Approach

**Good Stakeholder Identification Characteristics for the Healthcare Example**:
- **Comprehensive**: Included patients, caregivers, clinical staff, administrative staff, IT, regulators, and vendors
- **Differentiated**: Recognized that patients, physicians' offices, and pharmacies experience the refill process differently
- **Context-aware**: Considered how elderly patients often rely on family members for medication management
- **Influence-mapped**: Identified that while patients experience the problem, physicians' offices often initiate the process
- **Regulatory-inclusive**: Included FDA, DEA, and state pharmacy boards whose requirements significantly impact design
- **Workflow-oriented**: Understood how refills move through multiple hands and systems
- **Evidence-based**: Based on actual interviews and observations rather than assumptions
- **Actionable**: Provided clear guidance for who to involve in design and testing
- **Balanced**: Considered both clinical needs (patient safety) and operational needs (efficiency, cost)
- **Forward-looking**: Considered how changing regulations or technologies might affect stakeholders

## 13. Bad Approach

**Poor Stakeholder Identification Examples and Why They Fail**:

*"Only patients matter because they're the ones refilling prescriptions."*
- **Failure reason**: Ignores the complex workflow involving multiple parties
- **Missing**: Clinical staff who process refills, insurance companies that authorize them, family members who assist
- **Consequence**: Solutions that work in isolation but fail in real-world clinical workflow

*"We only need to talk to our power users - they'll tell us what everyone needs."*
- **Failure reason**: Assumes homogeneity and ignores edge cases or less vocal groups
- **Missing**: Elderly patients, low-tech users, new patients, patients with complex regimens
- **Consequence**: Solutions that work for sophisticated users but exclude or frustrate others

*"Stakeholders are whoever attends our monthly steering committee."*
- **Failure reason**: Confuses governance with stakeholdership; misses those affected but not decision-makers
- **Missing**: Frontline staff actually doing the work, patients experiencing the service, regulators setting rules
- **Consequence**: Solutions that please executives but fail in practice or violate regulations

*"Anyone who uses the system is a stakeholder - that's it."*
- **Failure reason**: Overly simplistic; doesn't differentiate between types and degrees of impact
- **Missing**: Understanding of how different stakeholders experience the problem differently
- **Consequence**: One-size-fits-all solutions that inadequately serve any particular group

*"We don't need to identify stakeholders - we just need to build what's requested."*
- **Failure reason**: Treats symptoms as problems and ignores root causes and systemic effects
- **Missing**: Understanding of why the request was made, who benefits, who might be negatively impacted
- **Consequence**: Building exactly what was asked for but not solving the underlying problem or creating new issues

## 14. Common Mistakes

- **Assuming Homogeneity**: Treating all users or stakeholders as having the same needs, characteristics, or experiences
- **Focusing Only on the Obvious**: Missing secondary effects, indirect impacts, or less visible stakeholders
- **Confusing Stakeholders with Users**: Forgetting that people who don't directly use a system can still be significantly affected
- **Over-indexing on Vocal Minorities**: Letting the loudest complainers define the problem while ignoring silent majority
- **Ignoring Power Dynamics**: Not recognizing that some stakeholders have disproportionate influence on decisions
- **Static Thinking**: Treating stakeholder identification as a one-time activity rather than an ongoing process
- **Overlooking Edge Cases**: Missing users with disabilities, different cultural contexts, or unusual usage patterns
- **Failing to Validate**: Creating stakeholder lists based on assumptions without checking with actual stakeholders
- **Neglecting Relationships**: Not understanding how stakeholders interact, influence each other, or hand off work
- **Ignoring Temporal Aspects**: Not recognizing that stakeholder involvement and needs may change over time
- **Assuming Representation**: Believing that one person can accurately represent an entire diverse stakeholder group
- **Overcomplicating**: Creating overly detailed stakeholder analyses that don't inform decisions or actions
- **Underestimating Effort**: Not allocating sufficient time for proper stakeholder identification and engagement
- **Missing Organizational Context**: Not understanding how stakeholder roles fit within larger organizational structures
- **Ignoring Negative Stakeholders**: Failing to identify people who might resist changes or have competing interests
- **Confusing Interest with Impact**: Not distinguishing between stakeholders who care about the problem and those actually affected by it

## 15. Security Considerations

Security considerations in stakeholder identification:
- Ensuring that stakeholder discovery activities don't inadvertently expose sensitive information about users or systems
- Considering whether certain stakeholders pose security risks (e.g., potential bad actors who might exploit the problem)
- Identifying if stakeholders have different security clearance levels or access requirements
- Understanding if stakeholder identification itself could create privacy concerns (e.g., tracking who is affected by a sensitive problem)
- Determining if stakeholder communication requires secure channels for sensitive discussions
- Considering whether stakeholder characteristics (like role or access level) have security implications
- Identifying if stakeholders are subject to different regulatory or compliance requirements affecting security
- Understanding if stakeholder access needs could conflict with security principles (least privilege, separation of duties)
- Considering whether stakeholder training or education needs have security components
- Determining if stakeholder feedback mechanisms could be exploited for social engineering or information gathering

## 16. Performance Considerations

Performance considerations in stakeholder identification:
- Understanding if different stakeholder groups experience performance impacts differently
- Identifying if stakeholder characteristics (like location or connection type) affect their perception of performance
- Ensuring that stakeholder engagement activities don't inadvertently impact system performance being measured
- Considering whether stakeholder technical proficiency affects their ability to perceive or describe performance issues
- Understanding if stakeholder goals related to performance (like throughput vs. latency) might conflict
- Determining if stakeholder communication about performance requires specific terminology or metrics
- Considering whether stakeholder training or documentation needs vary based on technical background
- Identifying if stakeholder success criteria include performance thresholds or expectations
- Understanding if stakeholder locations or access methods create different performance characteristics
- Determining if stakeholder feedback about performance needs to be weighted or segmented by user type

## 17. Scalability Considerations

Scalability considerations for stakeholder identification:
- Determining if stakeholder characteristics change at different scales (e.g., different needs at 100 vs 100K users)
- Identifying if stakeholder segmentation strategies need to evolve as the user base grows
- Understanding if stakeholder communication methods that work for small groups fail at scale
- Determining if stakeholder influence and interest distributions change with scale (e.g., power law effects)
- Considering whether stakeholder characteristics become more or less homogeneous at scale
- Understanding if edge case stakeholders become more significant or less significant at scale
- Determining if stakeholder feedback mechanisms need to scale differently than the core system
- Considering whether stakeholder success criteria should include scalability thresholds or expectations
- Understanding if stakeholder onboarding or training needs change as the system grows
- Identifying if stakeholder segmentation for targeted solutions needs to evolve with scale

## 18. Maintainability Considerations

Maintainability considerations in stakeholder identification:
- Ensuring that stakeholder identification methods are sustainable over time as the system evolves
- Creating stakeholder documentation that remains useful as personnel and roles change
- Building stakeholder engagement processes that can be repeated as the system evolves
- Understanding if stakeholder characteristics are likely to change over time and how to track those changes
- Considering whether stakeholder identification should account for planned system evolution or changes
- Determining if stakeholder feedback channels should be permanent fixtures or temporary engagements
- Considering whether stakeholder characteristics should influence long-term architectural decisions
- Understanding if stakeholder retirement or turnover requires knowledge transfer processes
- Determining if stakeholder identification should inform ongoing maintenance and support planning
- Considering whether stakeholder characteristics should influence deprecation or migration planning
- Understanding if stakeholder feedback loops should be built into ongoing system operations
- Determining if stakeholder identification should be treated as a continuous improvement activity rather than a one-time task

## 19. Junior Developer Approach

**How Junior Developers Typically Approach Stakeholder Identification**:
- Often assume that the users mentioned in the initial request are the only stakeholders
- Tend to focus exclusively on primary users while ignoring secondary or indirect effects
- May confuse stakeholders with decision-makers or people who control budgets
- Frequently rely on assumptions or stereotypes rather than actual stakeholder engagement
- Often overlook stakeholders who don't use the system directly but are affected by its outcomes
- May not recognize that different user segments can have substantially different needs
- Tend to treat stakeholder identification as a paperwork exercise rather than an investigative process
- Often fail to validate their stakeholder assumptions with actual stakeholders
- May not understand the importance of stakeholder relationships and influence dynamics
- Frequently miss stakeholders who represent compliance, legal, or regulatory interests
- Tend to focus on current state without considering how stakeholders might change over time

**What Juniors Should Learn**:
- Practice systems thinking to understand how problems affect interconnected parties
- Develop active listening skills to uncover stakeholders through conversations
- Learn to distinguish between users, stakeholders, and affected parties
- Understand the value of extreme or edge case users in revealing system weaknesses
- Practice mapping stakeholder relationships and influence networks
- Learn techniques for identifying stakeholders that aren't immediately obvious
- Understand that stakeholder identification is an iterative process of discovery and validation
- Develop empathy for stakeholders with different backgrounds, technical levels, and priorities
- Learn to document stakeholder characteristics in ways that inform design decisions
- Practice validating stakeholder assumptions through direct engagement rather than relying on secondhand information
- Understand how to balance the needs of competing stakeholder groups
- Learn to identify stakeholders who might resist changes or have competing interests

## 20. Senior Developer Approach

**How Senior Developers Think About Stakeholder Identification**:
- Automatically consider the broader ecosystem and ripple effects of any problem
- Immediately think about secondary and tertiary effects beyond the obvious primary users
- Consider stakeholder identification as an investigative process rather than a labeling exercise
- Think about power dynamics, influence patterns, and potential conflicts between stakeholder groups
- Consider how stakeholder characteristics might change over time or under different circumstances
- Think about the longitudinal aspects of stakeholder relationships and evolving needs
- Consider stakeholder identification through multiple lenses (organizational, technical, political, etc.)
- Think about how to balance competing stakeholder needs when they conflict
- Consider the ethical implications of stakeholder inclusion or exclusion
- Think about how stakeholder identification informs not just solution design but also implementation and change management
- Consider how stakeholder characteristics might affect long-term maintenance and evolution of the system

**What Seniors Do**:
- Use techniques like "follow the money" or "follow the work" to identify less obvious stakeholders
- Apply network analysis concepts to understand stakeholder relationships and influence flows
- Ensure stakeholder identification considers both current state and future scenarios
- Balance comprehensiveness with practicality - identify enough stakeholders to inform decisions without paralysis
- Use stakeholder characteristics to segment research, design, and testing efforts appropriately
- Recognize that stakeholder identification is as much about understanding relationships as it is about listing parties
- Document stakeholder assumptions and uncertainties explicitly
- Use stakeholder maps as communication tools to help teams understand theproblem context
- Regularly revisit and validate stakeholder understanding throughout the project lifecycle
- Consider stakeholder identification as a foundational activity that enables other work rather than a box to check
- Balance the needs of organized stakeholder groups with the insights from individual/stories
- Understand that stakeholder identification requires different techniques for different types of stakeholders (users vs regulators vs internal teams)

## 21. Senior Engineer Questions

Senior engineers should ask when identifying stakeholders:
- Who experiences this problem most directly, and whose experience might we be missing?
- Who benefits from the problem persisting (possibly creating perverse incentives)?
- Who has implicit or explicit authority over decisions related to this problem?
- Who might resist changes to the current situation, even if those changes are beneficial overall?
- Who are the "invisible" stakeholders whose work enables or is affected by the problem but who aren't obvious?
- How do stakeholder characteristics vary across different segments, and what implications does this have for solution design?
- What relationships exist between stakeholders, and how do they influence each other's perspectives and behaviors?
- What assumptions are we making about stakeholder needs, characteristics, or behaviors?
- How might stakeholder involvement or importance change over the course of solving this problem?
- What stakeholder characteristics create special considerations (access needs, communication preferences, regulatory constraints)?
- Who are the expert stakeholders who understand the deeper context or history of this problem?
- How do stakeholder goals and success criteria align or conflict with each other?
- What stakeholder characteristics might create implementation, deployment, or adoption challenges?
- Who are the stakeholders most likely to be overlooked in typical analyses?
- How do we validate our stakeholder understanding without relying solely on self-reported information?
- What stakeholder characteristics should influence technical architectural decisions?
- How do we balance the need for comprehensive stakeholder identification with practical project constraints?

## 22. Practical Exercise

**Exercise**: Identify stakeholders for a hypothetical situation.

**Scenario**: A university wants to implement a new system for managing graduate student thesis submission and review process.

**Instructions**:
1. Look beyond the obvious students and faculty advisors
2. Consider all the people, systems, and organizations that interact with or are affected by this process
3. Think about different types of graduate students (PhD vs Masters, different departments, international vs domestic)
4. Consider the full lifecycle from thesis drafting to final archiving
5. Think about both internal university processes and external requirements

**Task**: Create a stakeholder list categorizing who has involvement with or is affected by the thesis submission process.
Then:
- Identify primary users vs. secondary users vs. internal stakeholders vs. external stakeholders
- Note any stakeholders who might have competing interests or conflicting needs
- Identify stakeholders who might be easy to overlook
- Determine which stakeholders have the most influence vs. who are most affected
- Consider how stakeholder characteristics might vary (technical proficiency, availability, urgency, etc.)
- Identify any regulatory or compliance requirements that create stakeholder obligations
- Think about how stakeholder identification would influence solution design decisions
- Consider what engagement strategies would work best for different stakeholder groups
- Identify any stakeholder characteristics that would create special considerations for the solution

## 23. Definition of Done

Stakeholder identification is considered complete when:
- [ ] All primary users who directly experience the problem have been identified
- [ ] All secondary users who are indirectly affected by the problem have been identified
- [ ] All internal stakeholders within the organization who are affected or involved have been identified
- [ ] All external stakeholders outside the organization who are affected or involved have been identified
- [ ] Stakeholder characteristics relevant to problem understanding and solution design have been documented
- [ ] Relationships and interactions between different stakeholder groups have been mapped
- [ ] Stakeholder influence, interest, and urgency have been assessed where relevant
- [ ] Assumptions made during stakeholder identification have been documented and noted for validation
- [ ] Gaps in stakeholder understanding have been identified and planned for further investigation
- [ ] Stakeholder identification has been sufficient to inform research, design, and solution exploration efforts
- [ ] The stakeholder list has been reviewed and validated with knowledgeable parties
- [ ] Stakeholder identification documentation is accessible and useful to the project team

## 24. Checklist

- [ ] Stakeholder identification looks beyond obvious users to indirect and secondary effects
- [ ] Different types of stakeholders (users, internal, external, regulators, etc.) have been considered
- [ ] Stakeholder lists include both beneficiaries and those who might be negatively impacted
- [ ] Stakeholder characteristics relevant to the problem (goals, pain points, workarounds, influence) have been documented
- [ ] Stakeholder identification considers power dynamics and influence patterns, not just numbers
- [ ] Edge case or extreme stakeholders have been considered (disabled users, minority groups, novel use cases)
- [ ] Stakeholder validation has occurred through direct engagement rather than just assumptions
- [ ] Stakeholder relationships and interactions have been mapped (who hands off work to whom, who influences whom)
- [ ] Temporal aspects have been considered (how stakeholder needs might change over time)
- [ ] Assumptions made during stakeholder identification are documented and noted for follow-up
- [ ] Stakeholder identification has been sufficient to guide research planning and participant recruitment
- [ ] Different stakeholder segments have been considered for tailored engagement approaches
- [ ] Stakeholder documentation is organized and accessible for use by the project team
- [ ] Stakeholder identification considers compliance, legal, and ethical stakeholders
- [ ] The effort invested in stakeholder identification is appropriate to the problem's importance and complexity
- [ ] Stakeholder identification has been treated as an iterative process rather than a one-time activity
