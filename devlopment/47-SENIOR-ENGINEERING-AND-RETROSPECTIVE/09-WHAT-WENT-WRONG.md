# 09 — What Went Wrong

## 1. What Is This?

"What Went Wrong" is a reflective practice focused on identifying problems, shortcomings, failures, and ineffective practices from a project, sprint, or time period. Unlike blame-oriented post-mortems, this approach seeks to understand what didn't work as expected, what caused frustration or inefficiency, and where efforts fell short of goals. The goal is not to assign fault but to learn from difficulties in order to prevent recurrence, improve processes, and build resilience. By honestly examining shortcomings, teams can address root causes rather than just symptoms and create meaningful opportunities for growth.

## 2. Why Does It Matter

Honestly examining what went wrong directly impacts:
- **Problem Prevention**: Identifying root causes helps avoid repeating the same mistakes
- **Process Improvement**: Reveals flaws in workflows, tools, or practices that need fixing
- **Risk Management**: Shows what vulnerabilities or weaknesses existed in the approach
- **Learning Acceleration**: Failures often teach more than successes when examined properly
- **Resource Optimization**: Prevents wasting effort on ineffective approaches or tools
- **Quality Enhancement**: Addresses gaps that led to defects, errors, or subpar outcomes
- **Team Resilience**: Builds capacity to handle adversity and recover from setbacks
- **Expectation Alignment**: Helps teams understand limitations and adjust planning accordingly
- **Innovation Safety**: Creates psychological safety to admit when experiments didn't work
- **Stakeholder Trust**: Demonstrates honesty and commitment to improvement
- **Technical Debt Awareness**: Highlights shortcuts or compromises that created future work
- **Skill Gap Identification**: Shows where training or hiring might be needed
- **Communication Improvement**: Exposes breakdowns in information sharing or understanding
- **Motivation Preservation**: Addresses frustrations that could lead to disengagement
- **Decision Refinement**: Improves future choices by learning from poor outcomes
- **Adaptability Building**: Increases capacity to adjust when things don't go as planned

## 3. Conducting Effective "What Went Wrong" Reflections

### Setting the Stage
- **Emphasize Learning, Not Blame**: Clearly state the purpose is understanding, not fault-finding
- **Psychological Safety**: Ensure team members feel safe to share shortcomings honestly
- **Confidentiality**: What's shared in this context stays in this context unless agreed otherwise
- **Facilitation Preparedness**: Have strategies ready to deflect blame and maintain focus on systems
- **Time Allocation**: Dedicate sufficient time for meaningful exploration (often 20-40 minutes)
- **Individual Preparation**: Allow time for private reflection before group discussion
- **Evidence Mindset**: Encourage bringing specific examples or data, not just impressions

### Individual Reflection
- **Silent Writing**: Begin with quiet individual thinking to generate honest thoughts
- **Specific Prompting**: Use targeted questions to move beyond vague dissatisfaction
- **Impact Focus**: Consider not just what happened but what it cost or affected
- **Root Cause Tendency**: Try to think beyond surface symptoms to underlying causes
- **Timeboxing**: Limit individual reflection to maintain energy and focus for sharing
- **Documentation Preparedness**: Be ready to share specific observations, not just feelings

### Group Sharing
- **Structured Sharing**: Use techniques like round-robin to ensure everyone speaks
- **Clarification Questions**: Ask for specifics when statements are vague or general
- **Blame Interception**: Immediately reframe personal criticisms into systemic observations
- **Similarity Grouping**: Combine related issues to identify patterns and themes
- **Impact Exploration**: Discuss what each problem actually cost or affected
- **Evidence Anchoring**: Ask for examples, data, or concrete instances to support claims
- **Validation Without Agreement**: Acknowledge the experience without necessarily endorsing the interpretation
- **Visual Capture**: Use shared boards or screens to make issues visible to all

### Analysis and Synthesis
- **Pattern Recognition**: Look for common themes, timing patterns, or recurring elements
- **Systemic Thinking**: Consider how processes, tools, or structures contributed to issues
- **Root Cause Exploration**: Use techniques like Five Whys to dig deeper than surface problems
- **Context Examination**: What specific circumstances made these problems likely or worse?
- **Dependency Mapping**: How did issues in one area affect or create problems in others?
- **Prevention Potential**: Which issues seem most preventable with reasonable effort?
- **Learning Extraction**: What specific insights could help avoid similar problems?
- **Scope Clarification**: Distinguish between things within team control and external factors

### Documentation and Follow-up
- **Honest Capture**: Record problems clearly and specifically, avoiding vague labels
- **Cause Documentation**: When possible, note suspected or discovered root causes
- **Impact Statement**: Briefly capture what each problem actually cost or affected
- **Experiment Identification**: Derive specific, testable changes to address root causes
- **Ownership Assignment**: Designate who will follow up on investigating or addressing issues
- **Tracking Mechanism**: Establish how progress on addressing problems will be monitored
- **Transparency Decision**: Determine what should be shared with stakeholders beyond the team
- **Closure Criteria**: Define what would constitute adequate resolution or learning

## 4. Prompt Questions for "What Went Wrong"

### Project/Product Delivery
- What features or components failed to meet user needs or expectations?
- Which deliverables were late, incomplete, or of inadequate quality?
- What user feedback indicated dissatisfaction or problems?
- Which performance or quality metrics fell short of targets?
- What problems did we fail to solve adequately for our users?
- Which design decisions created usability, performance, or maintenance issues?
- What technical solutions proved inadequate or problematic in practice?
- What scope creep or gold plating occurred without sufficient benefit?
- Which accepted risks actually materialized into problems?
- What assumptions about users, technology, or environment proved incorrect?

### Process and Execution Problems
- Which meetings or ceremonies were consistently unproductive or wasteful?
- What communication practices led to misunderstandings, delays, or errors?
- Which estimation or planning techniques proved significantly inaccurate?
- What risk mitigation strategies failed or were inadequately applied?
- Which tools or technologies hindered rather than helped productivity?
- How did our collaboration practices create friction or conflict?
- What workflow inefficiencies caused unnecessary delays or rework?
- Which decision-making processes led to poor outcomes or regret?
- How did we mishandle dependencies, blockers, or external delays?
- What reporting or tracking mechanisms failed to provide needed visibility?
- Which gatekeeping or approval processes created unnecessary bottlenecks?
- How did our definition of done or acceptance criteria prove inadequate?

### Team and Interpersonal Issues
- Where did team members experience ineffective support during challenges?
- What knowledge sharing gaps led to duplicated effort or mistakes?
- Which conflicts were handled poorly or allowed to fester?
- Where did psychological safety break down, preventing honest communication?
- What instances of unclear responsibility or accountability occurred?
- How did we fail to recognize or appreciate contributions adequately?
- What workload imbalances led to burnout or resentment?
- Which leadership behaviors had negative impact or created problems?
- How did we poorly integrate new team members or transfers?
- What inclusion or equity issues surfaced during the period?
- Where did we fail to establish or maintain effective working agreements?
- How did stress or pressure bring out unhelpful behaviors in the team?

### Technical Shortcomings
- Which code quality practices were inconsistently applied or ignored?
- What testing strategy gaps allowed defects to reach users?
- Which refactoring efforts were neglected despite clear technical debt?
- What architectural decisions created scaling, performance, or maintenance problems?
- How did our DevOps or deployment practices fail or create issues?
- What performance bottlenecks emerged that we failed to address adequately?
- Which security vulnerabilities or concerns were identified but not resolved?
- How did our monitoring or observability fail to detect issues in time?
- What technical debt accumulation went unaddressed despite known problems?
- Which learning or skill development initiatives failed to build needed capabilities?
- What tooling or infrastructure choices proved inadequate or problematic?
- How did we fail to handle error cases, edge conditions, or failure modes?
- What integration points with external systems proved fragile or unreliable?

### Stakeholder and Business Misfits
- Where did we fail to manage stakeholder expectations effectively?
- What feedback loops with customers or users broke down or proved inadequate?
- Which business outcomes did we negatively impact or fail to influence?
- How did we poorly balance competing priorities or resource demands?
- What experiments or tests gave misleading results or were improperly conducted?
- How did we fail to anticipate or respond to changing requirements or conditions?
- What cost overruns or resource inefficiencies occurred without sufficient justification?
- How did we fail to advance certain strategic goals or initiatives despite effort?
- Which partnerships or collaborations failed to deliver expected value?
- Where did we poorly demonstrate business value, ROI, or stewardship of resources?
- What regulatory, compliance, or governance requirements were not met?

## 5. Common Challenges and How to Address Them

### Blame-Game Dynamics
- **Problem**: Conversation quickly shifts to who is at fault rather than what happened
- **Solutions**:
  - Immediately reframe "X messed up" to "what happened was..." and focus on systems
  - Use neutral language: "What occurred" instead of "what X did wrong"
  - Ask "what allowed this to happen?" rather than "who caused this?"
  - Emphasize that everyone makes mistakes; focus on preventing recurrence
  - Share personal examples of your own errors to model vulnerability
  - Use "we" language to emphasize collective responsibility
  - Separate the act from the actor: focus on behavior, not character
  - Have prepared phrases to redirect blame conversations: "Let's put that aside and look at what we can learn"

### Vague or Generalized Complaints
- **Problem**: Statements like "everything was chaotic" or "nothing worked" without specifics
- **Solutions**:
  - Ask for specific examples, instances, or occurrences
  - Request details: who, what, when, where, and how it manifested
  - Probe for impact: "How did that specifically affect our work or outcomes?"
  - Use the "Five Whys" technique to drill down from general statements
  - Ask what specific evidence supports the general impression
  - Request that people bring one concrete example to share
  - Use timelines or retrospectives to ground discussions in specific periods
  - Ask what would have been different if this particular thing had worked well

### Discomfort with Negativity or Criticism
- **Problem**: Team members avoid mentioning problems to maintain harmony
- **Solutions**:
  - Normalize that discussing problems is professional, not disloyal
  - Emphasize that identifying issues is the first step to fixing them
  - Frame as investment in future effectiveness, not dwelling on negatives
  - Start with appreciations to build safety before exploring problems
  - Share that even high-performing teams have issues to address
  - Use anonymous collection methods initially to build trust
  - Emphasize that the goal is learning, not venting or complaining
  - Connect problem identification to specific improvement experiments

### Symptom-Focused Rather Than Root Cause Analysis
- **Problem**: Team stops at describing what went wrong without exploring why
- **Solutions**:
  - Consistently ask "why did this happen?" or "what allowed this to occur?"
  - Use structured root cause techniques like Five Whys or fishbone diagrams
  - Ask what would need to be true for this problem to persist
  - Consider multiple potential causes rather than settling on the first explanation
  - Ask what changes would prevent this specific issue from recurring
  - Look for patterns across multiple similar problems to find common causes
  - Consider what systems, processes, or tools enabled or contributed to the issue
  - Ask what assumptions made this outcome likely or unsurprising in hindsight

### Overwhelm from Too Many Problems
- **Problem**: Team feels paralyzed by the number or severity of issues identified
- **Solutions**:
  - Use voting or grouping to identify 1-3 highest priority issues to focus on
  - Apply the "15% solution" principle: what small portion could team influence?
  - Distinguish between what team can control versus external factors
  - Separate urgent, high-impact issues from chronic, lower-priority concerns
  - Create a backlog of issues to address over time rather than expecting immediate fixes
  - Identify "quick wins" that could build momentum for tackling harder problems
  - Acknowledge that not everything can be fixed immediately, but we can choose where to start
  - Focus on problems whose resolution would prevent other issues from occurring

### Failure to Move Beyond Discussion to Action
- **Problem**: Team identifies problems but doesn't derive specific changes to try
- **Solutions**:
  - Consistently ask: "What specific experiment could we try to address this?"
  - Ensure action items are specific, testable, and owned by someone
  - Use "how might we" questions to reframe problems as design challenges
  - Identify what would constitute evidence that an attempted fix worked
  - Connect problems to potential process, tool, or practice changes
  - Assign owners to investigate or prototype solutions to identified problems
  - Schedule specific follow-up to check on progress addressing issues
  - Celebrate attempts to address problems, not just successful resolutions

## 6. Integrating "What Went Wrong" into Regular Practice

### In Retrospectives
- **Dedicated Exploration**: Allocate sufficient time for honest exploration of problems
- **Balanced with Positives**: Pair with "what went well" to maintain perspective
- **Varied Facilitation**: Use different techniques to avoid falling into complaint patterns
- **Trend Tracking**: Monitor whether similar problems recur across periods
- **Action Connection**: Always link problem identification to specific improvement experiments
- **Evidence Standard**: Encourage bringing data or specific examples, not just impressions
- **Follow-up Commitment**: Reserve time in next retrospective to review progress on identified issues

### Beyond Retrospectives
- **Immediate Issue Capture**: Create easy ways to log problems as they occur
- **Blame-Free Reporting**: Ensure incident reports focus on learning, not fault-finding
- **Code Review Culture**: Normalize discussing what's hard or confusing, not just what's wrong
- **Deployment Reflections**: Briefly note what was challenging or didn't go as planned
- **Technical Debt Tracking**: Make visible the accumulation of known shortcomings
- **Skill Gap Logging**: Track where team feels inadequate to tasks or challenges
- **Customer Feedback Systems**: Ensure negative feedback is captured and shared internally
- **Meeting Effectiveness Checks**: Regular brief assessments of what's not working in meetings
- **Tool Feedback Channels**: Create ways to report when tools hinder rather than help
- **Project Health Dashboards**: Include metrics on recurring issues or impediments
- **Learning From Failure Sessions**: Dedicated time to analyze significant setbacks

### Organizational Practices
- **Blameless Post-Mortem Policy**: Formal commitment to learning-oriented incident reviews
- **Knowledge Bases**: Tag and organize problem patterns and solutions discovered
- **Leadership Modeling**: Leaders openly discussing their own mistakes and learnings
- **Process Audits**: Regular reviews specifically looking for where things break down
- **Training Needs Analysis**: Use identified problems to guide professional development
- **Tool Evaluation Cycles**: Regular assessment of whether tools are helping or hurting
- **Communication Audits**: Periodic reviews of where information flow fails
- **Psychological Safety Measurements**: Track whether people feel safe to raise problems
- **Innovation Tolerance**: Measure how well organization handles experiments that don't work
- **Continuous Improvement Metrics**: Track whether identified problems actually improve over time

## 7. Scaling "What Went Wrong" Analysis Across Contexts

### For Individuals
- **Personal Problem Logs**: Regularly note what didn't work in your work or efforts
- **Decision Journals**: Record choices that led to poor outcomes and what you learned
- **Skill Gap Tracking**: Specifically note where you felt inadequate or unprepared
- **Energy Drains**: Identify tasks or interactions that consistently leave you depleted
- **Failure Analysis**: Systematically examine personal setbacks for patterns or lessons
- **Feedback Reflection**: When receiving criticism, focus on what you can learn rather than defending
- **Experimentation Logs**: Record what personal experiments failed and why
- **Mentoring Moments**: Identify times when attempts to help others were ineffective
- **Career Reflections**: Periodically assess what aspects of work consistently frustrate or challenge you
- **Boundary Issues**: Note where you failed to maintain healthy limits or said yes when you should have said no

### For Distributed Teams
- **Async Problem Collection**: Allow time-zone-flexible input before synchronous discussion
- **Digital Issue Tracking**: Use shared tools to log and categorize problems as they arise
- **Video Communication Care**: Be extra attentive to potential misunderstandings in virtual settings
- **Structured Virtual Turn-Taking**: Explicitly manage who shares when to prevent domination
- **Time Zone Sensitivity**: Rotate meeting times to share burden of inconvenient hours
- **Connection Problem Awareness**: Specifically note when technical issues hindered communication
- **Isolation Awareness**: Ask about feelings of disconnection or lack of support
- **Documentation Gaps**: Note when lack of shared information caused problems locally
- **Follow-up Message Discipline**: Ensure action items from virtual meetings are clearly communicated
- **Virtual Fatigue Tracking**: Note when virtual meeting formats themselves became problematic

### For Cross-Functional Groups
- **Language and Terminology Gaps**: Specifically note when misunderstandings arose from different jargon
- **Priority Misalignment**: Identify when different functions had conflicting senses of urgency or importance
- **Handoff Problems**: Focus on where work transferred poorly between functions or teams
- **Assumption Collisions**: Identify when different groups made incompatible assumptions about the work
- **Process Integration Issues**: Note where cross-functional processes created friction or delays
- **Communication Channel Mismatches**: Identify when preferred communication methods didn't align
- **Success Definition Differences**: Explicitly discuss what "done" or "successful" meant to each function
- **Resource Competition Problems**: Surface when groups competed for limited attention or resources
- **Decision-Making Process Clashes**: Note when different decision styles created confusion or delay
- **Escalation Path Problems**: Identify when it was unclear how or when to seek help from other functions

### For Leadership Teams
- **Strategic Missteps**: Focus on organizational-level decisions that didn't produce expected returns
- **Systemic Problem Recognition**: Identify when policies, structures, or processes created widespread issues
- **Stakeholder Misalignment**: Note when leadership actions failed to meet key constituency needs
- **Resource Misallocation**: Identify where investments of time, money, or talent failed to pay off
- **Culture Erosion Indicators**: Observe when behaviors inconsistent with desired culture increased
- **Leadership Gap Identification**: Specifically note where leadership capabilities fell short of needs
- **Innovation Stifling**: Identify when leadership actions unintentionally discouraged experimentation
- **Communication Breakdowns**: Track when leadership messages failed to inform or inspire as intended
- **Talent Management Failures**: Note where development, retention, or succession efforts fell short
- **Risk Management Lapses**: Identify when anticipated problems actually occurred despite mitigation efforts
- **Decision Process Problems**: Examine when leadership decision-making led to poor organizational outcomes
- **Adaptability Shortcomings**: Identify when organization was slow to respond to changing circumstances

## 8. Measuring the Impact of Honest Problem Examination

### Leading Indicators
- **Problem Specificity**: Movement from vague complaints to concrete, detailed observations
- **Blame Language Reduction**: Decrease in "who messed up" statements versus "what happened" observations
- **Root Cause Exploration**: Increase in discussions that go beyond symptoms to underlying causes
- **Action Derivation**: Percentage of identified problems that lead to specific improvement experiments
- **Participation Breadth**: Percentage of team regularly contributing honest problem identification
- **Psychological Safety Signals**: Comfort discussing both personal and team shortcomings
- **Follow-up Rigor**: Consistency in checking whether attempted fixes actually addressed issues
- **Learning Artifacts**: Documents, presentations, or other capture of insights from problems

### Lagging Indicators
- **Recurrence Reduction**: Measurable decrease in frequently discussed problems over time
- **Resolution Velocity**: Average time from problem identification to adequate resolution
- **Incident Severity Trend**: Whether major incidents are becoming less frequent or severe
- **Process Improvement Adoption**: Speed at which remedies for identified problems spread
- **Customer Satisfaction Trends**: Correlation with reduction in user-reported problems
- **Team Retention**: Whether teams practicing honest examination show better retention
- **Innovation Metrics**: Whether teams feel safer to experiment knowing failures will be examined productively
- **Knowledge Sharing Increase**: More documentation or teaching around what was learned from problems
- **Meeting Transformation**: Less dread, more willingness to engage in difficult conversations
- **Decision Quality Improvement**: Fewer repeated poor choices as learning accumulates
- **Resource Efficiency**: Less waste from repeatedly trying approaches that have been shown not to work
- **Psychological Health**: Lower burnout, higher resilience measurements linked to effective coping

### Qualitative Evidence
- **Narrative Transformation**: Stories of how examining specific problems led to meaningful change
- **Behavioral Shifts**: Noticeable increases in proactive problem reporting rather than waiting for retrospection
- **Language Evolution**: Team discourse includes more systems thinking and less personal criticism
- **Prevention Focus**: More effort directed toward avoiding problems rather than just fixing them
- **Cross-Team Invitations**: Other teams seeking to learn from how this team handles difficulties
- **Leadership Notice**: Leaders commenting on improved team maturity in handling challenges
- **Troubleshooting Sophistication**: More methodical, less reactive responses when issues arise
- **Meeting Transformation**: Difficult conversations become expected and valued parts of work rhythm
- **Identity Evolution**: Team begins seeing itself as capable of facing and learning from adversity
- **Innovation Resilience**: Ability to continue experimenting despite occasional failed attempts

## 9. Special Applications of Examining What Went Wrong

### Post-Incident Analyses
- **Immediate Response Evaluation**: What worked or didn't work in the initial response?
- **Detection Failures**: What monitoring, alerting, or observation failed to catch the issue early?
- **Communication Breakdowns**: Where did information sharing fail during the incident?
- **Decision Points**: What choices made during the incident helped or hindered resolution?
- **Resource Mobilization**: What delays or problems occurred in getting needed people or tools?
- **Knowledge Gaps**: What information or expertise was missing when it was most needed?
- **Tool Failures**: What systems, scripts, or automation failed or behaved unexpectedly?
- **Process Deviations**: Where did responders deviate from runbooks, and why was that necessary or harmful?
- **Escalation Issues**: What problems occurred in getting additional help or expertise when needed?
- **Recovery Complications**: What made service restoration more difficult or time-consuming than expected?
- **Stakeholder Management**: What external communications were inadequate or counterproductive?
- **Documentation Problems**: What missing, outdated, or incorrect information hindered response?
- **Psychological Impact**: What aspects of the incident response were particularly stressful or traumatic?
- **Prevention Opportunities**: What specific changes could prevent similar incidents or reduce impact?

### Process Improvement Initiatives
- **Bottleneck Identification**: Where does work consistently slow down, pile up, or wait?
- **Failure Points**: What specific steps in a process most often fail or produce errors?
- **Handoff Problems**: Where does work get dropped, misunderstood, or delayed when transferred?
- **Decision Latency**: Where do choices get stuck waiting for information, approval, or clarification?
- **Rework Origins**: What specific issues cause the most duplication of effort or fixing?
- **Communication Breakdowns**: Where does essential information fail to reach who needs it when needed?
- **Tool Friction Points**: What specific aspects of tools consistently slow down or frustrate users?
- **Batch vs. Flow Problems**: Where does processing in groups create issues that individual handling wouldn't?
- **Standards Conflicts**: Where do different teams or individuals apply rules inconsistently or problematically?
- **Feedback Loop Failures**: Where does information about outcomes fail to come back to inform future work?
- **Capacity Mismatches**: Where does demand regularly exceed processing capability or vice versa?
- **Transition Inefficiencies**: What makes shifting between different types of work particularly costly or problematic?
- **Metrics Blind Spots**: What important aspects of process performance are not measured or visible?
- **Training Effectiveness Gaps**: Where does preparedness regularly fall short of actual requirements?
- **Maintenance Debt Accumulation**: What shortcuts or deferrals create steadily increasing future work?

### Organizational Learning Initiatives
- **Strategic Misreadings**: Where did assumptions about market, technology, or competition prove wrong?
- **Investment Failures**: Where did allocated resources fail to deliver expected returns or benefits?
- **Policy Unintended Consequences**: What problems emerged from well-intentioned organizational rules?
- **Culture Clash Problems**: Where did efforts to change behavior actually increase friction or resistance?
- **Leadership Selection Outcomes**: What promotion or hiring decisions failed to produce expected capability?
- **Technology Adoption Problems**: What specific issues emerged when trying to implement new tools or platforms?
- **Change Management Breakdowns**: Where did efforts to transition actually make things worse temporarily?
- **Communication Success Gaps**: What messages or channels failed to reach or influence intended audiences?
- **Resource Allocation Inefficiencies**: Where did distributed resources end up less useful than anticipated?
- **Innovation Pipeline Clogs**: Where do promising ideas consistently fail to reach implementation stage?
- **Talent Development Gaps**: Where did training or experience programs fail to build needed capabilities?
- **Customer Misalignment**: Where did organizational efforts fail to meet actual user needs or preferences?
- **Regulatory Compliance Gaps**: Where did efforts to meet requirements actually fall short or create new risks?
- **Crisis Preparedness Shortfalls**: What specific weaknesses emerged when stress tests or incidents occurred?
- **Learning System Failures**: Where did systems meant to capture and share organizational learning actually fail?

## 10. Conclusion

Honestly examining what went wrong is not about dwelling on failure, assigning blame, or creating a culture of pessimism. Instead, it's about treating problems as valuable data points in the continuous learning process that characterizes high-performing teams and organizations. When teams develop the capacity to look squarely at shortcomings without fear or defensiveness, they transform inevitable setbacks into opportunities for growth, resilience, and increased capability. The most effective engineering teams don't avoid problems—they become skilled at diagnosing them accurately, learning from them meaningfully, and using that knowledge to build systems and practices that are increasingly resistant to similar issues. By making the examination of what went wrong a regular, disciplined practice rather than an occasional crisis response, teams create a culture where learning from difficulty is as natural and valued as celebrating success. In the complex, uncertain world of software engineering, where perfection is unattainable and learning is perpetual, the ability to productively engage with failure becomes one of the most important competitive advantages a team can possess.
